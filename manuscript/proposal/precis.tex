\head{The problem.}
The reachable set of an atmospheric entry vehicle, that is, every state it
can be driven to under admissible control, is what a safety analysis needs
bounded. Computing it exactly means solving a Hamilton--Jacobi--Isaacs
equation on a grid, which is exponential in the state dimension and capped
in practice near four or five states~\cite{bansal2017hamilton}. A
rigid-body entry vehicle carries thirteen states, and more once the heat
load, ablation and aerothermoelastic deformation are coupled, so the grid
method stops before the real problem starts. A polynomial barrier whose
coefficients come from an interior-point solver has analytical \emph{form},
but it inherits the solver's accuracy and is not by itself a certificate.
There are therefore two problems to solve, not one: the dimension of the
state, and the provenance of the guarantee. The vehicle and its flight
corridor enter only as data, so the bound sought is for hypersonic entry in
general and not for a particular vehicle.

\head{The approach.}
We embed the flight dynamics in a space of occupation measures. The
nonlinear flight mechanics $\dot x = f(x,c,t)$ become \emph{linear}: the
Liouville equation is a linear constraint on a measure $\mu$ over
$[0,T]\times\calX\times\calU$, and an outer bound on the reachable set is
certified by a dual pair of functions. So far this is the occupation-measure
program of Lasserre, Henrion and
collaborators~\cite{lasserre2008nonlinear,henrion2014convex}. Because the
aerodynamics are transcendental and known only to within tolerances, we
enclose them in intervals. The dynamics become a differential inclusion,
evaluated in validated interval arithmetic, with no polynomial recasting of
the model and no numerical optimization as the source of the guarantee.

The new content is what happens in the thin-atmosphere limit
$\eps = H_s/R_e\to 0$, where $H_s$ is the scale height of the atmosphere
and $R_e$ the radius of the planet. In this limit a trajectory separates
into three motions: arcs above the atmosphere, governed by gravity alone;
the equilibrium glide, on which lift balances weight less centrifugal
relief; and atmospheric \emph{passes}, in which the vehicle dips into dense
air, turns, and sheds a fixed share of its energy in a vanishing time. On
the flight corridor the occupation measures remain bounded and converge
along subsequences, but their limit forgets the passes: a measure weighs
each interval of time
by its length, and in the limit a pass has none. Since a reachable set is a
support and not an integral, what has no mass still counts.

We therefore read the loss where it occurs, on the energy dissipation.
Along every trajectory, for every control and every admissible model of
the aerodynamics, the mechanical energy in the rotating frame decreases at
the rate of the drag power, $\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -P\le 0$, so the total
dissipation is bounded uniformly in~$\eps$. We rescale time by
$\sqrt{\eps}$, as in the classical variables of
Chapman~\cite{chapman1959approximate}. Since the rescaled time axis is
unbounded, compactness is lost, and the concentration-compactness principle
of Lions~\cite{lions1984concentration1} classifies the normalized
dissipation densities into vanishing, compactness, or dichotomy. We expect
these to be the glide, a single pass, and passes that separate.

Rather than restore compactness, we extract the profile decomposition that
iterating the dichotomy provides, as in the theory of nonlinear dispersive
equations~\cite{bahouri1999high}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item a low-dimensional \emph{skeleton}, which is what survives the limit:
    the arcs above the atmosphere and the equilibrium glide;
  \item one \emph{boundary-layer bubble} per atmospheric pass, the analogue
    of a concentrating profile: the pass rescaled to its own width, whose
    net effect on the slow coordinates is a jump;
  \item a \emph{residual}, which is what the finitely many profiles do not
    capture, to be bounded explicitly in the corridor metric.
\end{itemize}
The number of bubbles is bounded \emph{a priori} by the energy budget
$\Delta\mathcal{E}$: a pass that dissipates at least $\delta$ can occur at
most $K_\delta\le\Delta\mathcal{E}/\delta$ times. Skeleton and bubbles together
form a limit system $\mathcal{H}_\delta$ in a few slow coordinates, which
flows along the arcs and the glide and jumps at each pass. The target
statement is
\[
  \calR_\eps(T) \;\subseteq\;
  \underbrace{\Pi\bigl(\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}(T)\bigr)}_{\text{skeleton and bubbles}}
  \;\oplus\;
  \underbrace{\B\bigl(\varrho(\eps,\delta)\bigr)}_{\text{residual}},
\]
uniformly over the admissible family generated by the uncertainty set, not
along a nominal trajectory. Here $\calR_\eps(T)$ is the reachable set of
the full system, $\Pi$ reads a state of the limit system back in the full
coordinates, and $\B(\varrho)$ is a ball in the corridor metric; Figure~1
illustrates the statement. The count $K_\delta$ and the radius $\varrho$
are to be computable in closed form from the corridor bounds and the
aerodynamic interval widths alone. The decomposition thus reduces a
reachability problem in thirteen or more states to one for a limit system
in a few slow coordinates. Compactness provides this structure but no
constants; those are to come from direct estimates: the energy identity, a
matching estimate at each pass, and averaging over the oscillations that do
not concentrate.

\head{Open problems.}
The central theorem is open, and this summary claims no result. The
following problems remain.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The limit.} To show that the limit of the occupation
    measures satisfies the Liouville equation of the limit system, with a
    jump term carried by the passes, uniformly over the admissible family.
  \item \textbf{Composition of passes.} To show that a trajectory with
    several passes factors through the individual passes and the flows
    between them, with an explicit matching remainder. The classical
    matched expansions give its form for one
    trajectory~\cite{shi1971skip}; the statement needed is uniform over the
    family.
  \item \textbf{Separation of scales.} The attitude and elastic motions do
    not concentrate. They are persistent oscillations, as is the
    oscillation about the glide, and in a profile decomposition such
    motions belong to the remainder~\cite{bahouri1999high}. The tool for
    them is averaging~\cite{sanders2007averaging}, but their frequencies
    cross during every pass, and the available results give orders and not
    bounds, and hold through a resonance only outside a set of small
    measure, which an outer bound cannot discard. What is needed is an
    averaging theorem with explicit constants, uniform over the admissible
    family and valid through resonance.
\end{enumerate}
The third problem is the critical path, and it is the part of the program
for which I am looking for an advisor.
