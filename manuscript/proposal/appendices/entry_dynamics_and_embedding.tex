\section{Coupled entry dynamics}
\label{apx:dyn}

The equations displayed in this appendix are stated once in computer
algebra, and what the appendix claims of them is reduced to identities. The
checks are of three standings. For the translational equations the check is
made against the same definition of the field that is evaluated in
floating point and in interval arithmetic, and it derives them
from Newton's law in the rotating frame rather than taking them on trust
from the literature. The attitude equations---the quaternion kinematics
with the planet's rotation carried into the body rate, Euler's equations,
the passage from the body force to the wind axes, and with it the glide
balance in its six-degree-of-freedom form---are checked against the
statement of them given here, and tied to the translational field by
showing that an attitude at incidence and bank returns the point-mass
field. The heating correlation and the modal oscillator are constitutive
laws; there is nothing to derive them from, and what is checked is what
follows from them. Two blocks are derived instead. The heat shield is
conservation of mass and of energy in depth, taken in \cref{sec:thermal}
from that form to the equation that is integrated, across the receding
surface, and onto the cells of the state; what stays constitutive in it is
the material and the chemistry of its surface, each named. And the force of
the air on the structural modes is derived in \cref{sec:piston} from the
equations of one-dimensional gas dynamics.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Coordinate rationale}\label{sec:coord-rationale}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The coordinate choice is dictated by two competing requirements of the
interval-arithmetic evaluation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The corridor must contract tightly.} The entry
    corridor---bounded altitude, bounded speed, and limits on heating
    rate, dynamic pressure and load factor---defines the region whose
    reachable subset we certify. In spherical position and wind-frame
    speed the first two are box constraints on states,
    $h_{\min}\le r - R_e \le h_{\max}$ and $V_{\min}\le V\le V_{\max}$, for
    which the interval hull is exact. The other three are not boxes: each
    is a curve $h \ge h_{\min}(V)$ in the altitude--speed plane, the load
    limit depending on attitude as well. But the dynamic pressure
    $\tfrac12\rho V^2$ and the stagnation heating rate of \cref{eq:Qdot}
    are each decreasing in altitude and increasing in speed, so the part of
    a box that satisfies the limit has an interval hull that is computed
    exactly, by solving the limit along two edges of the box, with no
    dependency loss. What a box cannot represent is the curved boundary
    itself, and that is recovered by subdivision.

  \item \textbf{The glide balance must be native.} The equilibrium-glide
    condition $\Lambda = 1$ relates altitude, speed and the vertical
    component of the lift; to leading order it does not involve the
    flight-path angle. Solved for the vertical lift fraction---the bank
    angle, in the point-mass model---it is a graph over~$(r,V)$, on which
    the flight-path angle is determined by $(r,V)$ at the next order. The
    profile decomposition---skeleton, bubbles, residual---is organized
    around this surface. Were it a derived nonlinear function of the states
    (as it would be in Cartesian position and velocity), every evaluation
    of the distance to it would pay a dependency loss.
\end{enumerate}

The translational state is therefore carried in spherical-LVLH coordinates
$(r,\lambda,\phi,V,\gamma,\psi)$. Attitude is carried as a unit quaternion
$\vvec{q} = (q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3)^\tr$ relating the body frame~$\mathcal{B}$ and
the ECEF frame~$\mathcal{E}$ (its direction-cosine matrix
$\mat{R}_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{B}} = \mat{R}(\vvec{q})$ is given in
\cref{eq:DCM})---this avoids all Euler-angle singularities and keeps the
quaternion kinematics in their simplest form.

The chart is singular at the geographic poles $\phi = \pm\pi/2$, and in two
places: the longitude rate~\cref{eq:lamdot}, through $1/\cos\phi$, and the
heading rate~\cref{eq:psidot}, through $\tan\phi$. Both are coordinate
singularities, not physical ones, but only the first is harmless.
Longitude is cyclic in this model---it enters neither the gravity field,
the corridor constraints nor the glide condition---whereas heading enters
the Coriolis and oblateness terms and cannot be integrated separately. The
corridor is therefore taken with $\abs{\phi}\le\phi_{\max}<\pi/2$. That is
a restriction on the trajectory class and is stated as one: a ground track
that overflies a pole needs a second chart, or the Cartesian formulation of
\cref{sec:summary-table}. Re-orienting the coordinate system to move the
pole away from the trajectory is not available, because the gravity field
and the rotation vector are axisymmetric about the geographic axis and no
other: in a tilted chart the eastward gravity component no longer vanishes
and $\vvec{\omega}_E$ acquires components along all three axes.

The Savageau--Voit polynomial recasting of the thermal subsystem is not
used. Because interval arithmetic evaluates transcendentals natively,
the density-square-root embedding $y = \sqrt{\rho/\rho_0}$ and the
polynomial surrogate for wall temperature are unnecessary, and the raw
physical variables are retained. The recasting survives only inside the
moment-sum-of-squares cross-check, which needs polynomial data.


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Frames}\label{sec:frames}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Rotation convention.}
Throughout, $\mat{R}_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}$ denotes the rotation that
carries coordinates \emph{from} frame~$\mathcal{B}$ \emph{to}
frame~$\mathcal{A}$, i.e.\ $\vvec{v}^{\mathcal{A}} =
\mat{R}_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}\,\vvec{v}^{\mathcal{B}}$; its columns are the
axes of~$\mathcal{B}$ expressed in~$\mathcal{A}$. Each such matrix is
orthogonal, so $\mat{R}_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}^{-1} =
\mat{R}_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}^{\tr} = \mat{R}_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}}$,
and subscripts compose in the usual way,
$\mat{R}_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}\mat{R}_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{C}} =
\mat{R}_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}}$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathcal{E}$ (\textbf{ECEF}): rotating with the constant
    planetary rate $\vvec{\omega}_E = \omega_E\,\hat{z}_{\mathcal{E}}$.

  \item $\mathcal{L}$ (\textbf{LVLH / NED}): at geocentric position
    $(r,\lambda,\phi)$, with basis vectors
    \begin{align*}
      \hat{e}_N &= -\sin\phi\cos\lambda\,\hat{x}_{\mathcal{E}}
                    -\sin\phi\sin\lambda\,\hat{y}_{\mathcal{E}}
                    +\cos\phi\,\hat{z}_{\mathcal{E}},\\
      \hat{e}_E &= -\sin\lambda\,\hat{x}_{\mathcal{E}}
                    +\cos\lambda\,\hat{y}_{\mathcal{E}},\\
      \hat{e}_D &= -\cos\phi\cos\lambda\,\hat{x}_{\mathcal{E}}
                    -\cos\phi\sin\lambda\,\hat{y}_{\mathcal{E}}
                    -\sin\phi\,\hat{z}_{\mathcal{E}}.
    \end{align*}
    The rotation $\mat{R}_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{L}}$ from $\mathcal{L}$ to
    $\mathcal{E}$ (columns are the above vectors in~$\mathcal{E}$) is a
    function of $(\lambda,\phi)$ alone.

  \item $\mathcal{W}$ (\textbf{Wind frame}): $x$-axis along the
    planet-relative velocity, third axis in the
    $\vvec{V}$--$\hat{e}_D$ plane, $y$-axis completing the right-handed
    system. The triad actually used below
    is~$(\hat{V},\hat{n}_\gamma,\hat{n}_\psi)$
    of~\crefrange{eq:Vhat}{eq:npsi}, in which $\hat{n}_\gamma$ points along
    increasing flight-path angle---radially \emph{outward} at level
    flight, $\hat{n}_\gamma|_{\gamma=0} = -\hat{e}_D$---so that a positive
    normal force appears as a positive $F_\gamma$ and the point-mass
    identification $F_\gamma = L\cos\sigma$ carries the expected sign.
    Parameterized by
    flight-path angle~$\gamma$ (positive climbing) and
    heading~$\psi$ (clockwise from North):
    \[
      \hat{V} = \cos\gamma\cos\psi\,\hat{e}_N
              + \cos\gamma\sin\psi\,\hat{e}_E
              - \sin\gamma\,\hat{e}_D.
    \]

  \item $\mathcal{B}$ (\textbf{Body frame}): fixed at the instantaneous
    centre of mass. Related to~$\mathcal{E}$ by the attitude quaternion
    $\vvec{q}$ through the direction-cosine matrix
    $\mat{R}_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{B}} = \mat{R}(\vvec{q})$, which carries
    body components to~$\mathcal{E}$
    ($\vvec{v}^{\mathcal{E}} = \mat{R}(\vvec{q})\,\vvec{v}^{\mathcal{B}}$):
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DCM}
  \mat{R}(\vvec{q})
  = \begin{pmatrix}
      q_0^2+q_1^2-q_2^2-q_3^2 & 2(q_1q_2-q_0q_3) & 2(q_1q_3+q_0q_2)\\
      2(q_1q_2+q_0q_3) & q_0^2-q_1^2+q_2^2-q_3^2 & 2(q_2q_3-q_0q_1)\\
      2(q_1q_3-q_0q_2) & 2(q_2q_3+q_0q_1) & q_0^2-q_1^2-q_2^2+q_3^2
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
quadratic in~$\vvec{q}$ (its columns are the body axes expressed
in~$\mathcal{E}$).


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{State vector}\label{sec:state-vector}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The full coupled state is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:state-full}
  x = \bigl(\underbrace{r,\lambda,\phi,V,\gamma,\psi}_{6},\;
       \underbrace{q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3,p,q_b,r_b}_{7},\;
       \underbrace{Q_{\mathrm{tot}},m,s,\,
                   T_1,\dots,T_{N_T},\,\rho_1,\dots,\rho_{N_T}}_{3+2N_T},\;
       \underbrace{\eta_1,\dots,\eta_{N_m},
                   \dot\eta_1,\dots,\dot\eta_{N_m}}_{2N_m}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with the unit-quaternion constraint $\norm{\vvec{q}}=1$ making the
translational-rotational block effectively 12-dimensional. The third block
is the heat load, the mass, and the heat shield resolved in depth on $N_T$
cells~(\cref{sec:thermal}): its recession, and a temperature and a density
in each cell. Total dimension: $16 + 2N_T + 2N_m$.


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Translational kinematics}\label{sec:trans-kin}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The planet-relative velocity in the $\mathcal{L}$ (NED) frame is
$\vvec{V}_{\mathcal{L}} =
(V\cos\gamma\cos\psi,\;V\cos\gamma\sin\psi,\;-V\sin\gamma)^\tr$. The
kinematic equations are:
\begin{align}
  \dot r      &= V\sin\gamma, \label{eq:rdot}\\
  \dot\lambda &= \frac{V\cos\gamma\sin\psi}{r\cos\phi},
                                                         \label{eq:lamdot}\\
  \dot\phi    &= \frac{V\cos\gamma\cos\psi}{r}.         \label{eq:phidot}
\end{align}


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Gravitational acceleration}\label{sec:gravity}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The $J_2$ gravitational potential in geocentric spherical coordinates is
\[
  \Phi_g(r,\phi)
  = -\frac{\mu}{r}\biggl[
      1 - \half J_2\Bigl(\frac{R_e}{r}\Bigr)^{\!2}
          \bigl(3\sin^2\!\phi - 1\bigr)
    \biggr].
\]
Its gradient, resolved in $\mathcal{L}$ (NED), has only radial and
northward components:
\begin{align}
  g_D(r,\phi) &= \frac{\mu}{r^2}\biggl[
    1 + \tfrac{3}{2}\,J_2\Bigl(\frac{R_e}{r}\Bigr)^{\!2}
        \bigl(1-3\sin^2\!\phi\bigr)
  \biggr], \label{eq:gD}\\[4pt]
  g_N(r,\phi) &= -\frac{3\mu}{r^2}\,J_2
    \Bigl(\frac{R_e}{r}\Bigr)^{\!2}
    \sin\phi\cos\phi, \label{eq:gN}\\
  g_E &= 0\quad\text{(by axial symmetry)}.\nonumber
\end{align}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:gravity-spherical}
This reproduces the Cartesian form under the
identifications $r_3/\norm{\vvec{r}} = \sin\phi$ and
$\norm{\vvec{r}} = r$, but the reduction is not a term-by-term match and
is worth doing explicitly. The Cartesian $J_2$ perturbation carries the
factor $\bigl(1-5(r_3/\norm{\vvec{r}})^2\bigr)$ on its two equatorial
components and $\bigl(3-5(r_3/\norm{\vvec{r}})^2\bigr)$ on its polar one;
projecting that vector onto~$\hat{r}$ and using
$\cos^2\!\phi + \sin^2\!\phi = 1$ collapses the pair to
\[
  \bigl(1-5\sin^2\!\phi\bigr)\cos^2\!\phi
  + \bigl(3-5\sin^2\!\phi\bigr)\sin^2\!\phi
  = 1-3\sin^2\!\phi ,
\]
which is the factor appearing in~\cref{eq:gD}. Carrying the Cartesian
$\bigl(1-5\sin^2\!\phi\bigr)$ into the radial component instead would
double the polar correction and move its sign change from
$\phi = \arcsin(1/\sqrt{3}) \approx 35.3^\circ$ to
$\arcsin(1/\sqrt{5}) \approx 26.6^\circ$. The spherical form makes the
interval evaluation tighter: $g_D$ and~$g_N$ are explicit functions of
$(r,\phi)$, with no square root of a sum of squares.
\end{remark}


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Translational dynamics}\label{sec:trans-dyn}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Newton's law in the rotating ECEF frame, resolved along the wind-frame
axes (tangential~$\hat{V}$, normal-in-vertical-plane, lateral), gives
the force equations. Define the aerodynamic force components:
\begin{align*}
  F_V     &= \text{force along } \hat{V},\\
  F_\gamma &= \text{force $\perp\hat{V}$, in the vertical plane},\\
  F_\psi  &= \text{force $\perp$ the vertical plane (lateral)}.
\end{align*}
In the 3-DOF model $F_V=-D$, $F_\gamma=L\cos\sigma$,
$F_\psi=L\sin\sigma$. In the full 6-DOF formulation these are
\emph{derived from the attitude quaternion}---see
\cref{sec:aero-force}.

The translational dynamical equations are:
\begin{align}
  \dot V &= \frac{F_V}{m}
    - g_D\sin\gamma + g_N\cos\gamma\cos\psi
    + \omega_E^2 r\cos\phi
      \bigl[\sin\gamma\cos\phi - \cos\gamma\cos\psi\sin\phi\bigr],
    \label{eq:Vdot}
  \\[6pt]
  \dot\gamma &= \frac{1}{V}\biggl\{
    \frac{F_\gamma}{m}
    - \Bigl(g_D - \frac{V^2}{r}\Bigr)\cos\gamma
    - g_N\sin\gamma\cos\psi
    + 2\omega_E V\cos\phi\sin\psi \nonumber\\
    &\qquad{}+ \omega_E^2 r\cos\phi
      \bigl[\cos\gamma\cos\phi + \sin\gamma\cos\psi\sin\phi\bigr]
    \biggr\}, \label{eq:gamdot}
  \\[6pt]
  \dot\psi &= \frac{1}{V\cos\gamma}\biggl\{
    \frac{F_\psi}{m}
    + \frac{V^2}{r}\cos^2\!\gamma\,\sin\psi\tan\phi
    - g_N\sin\psi \nonumber\\
    &\qquad{}+ 2\omega_E V
      \bigl[\sin\phi\cos\gamma - \sin\gamma\cos\phi\cos\psi\bigr]
    + \omega_E^2 r\cos\phi\sin\phi\sin\psi
    \biggr\}. \label{eq:psidot}
\end{align}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:vinh-reduction}
With $g_N = 0$ (spherical gravity) and
$\omega_E = 0$ (non-rotating planet), these reduce to the classical Vinh
equations for unpowered entry~\cite{vinh1980hypersonic}:
\[
  \dot V = \frac{F_V}{m} - g\sin\gamma,\qquad
  V\dot\gamma = \frac{F_\gamma}{m} - \Bigl(g-\frac{V^2}{r}\Bigr)\cos\gamma,
  \qquad
  V\cos\gamma\,\dot\psi = \frac{F_\psi}{m}
    + \frac{V^2}{r}\cos^2\!\gamma\,\sin\psi\tan\phi .
\]
This reduction is the working check on the signs
in~\crefrange{eq:Vdot}{eq:psidot}, and it is worth performing rather than
assuming: the three equations are assembled by projecting Newton's law onto
the wind triad~\crefrange{eq:Vhat}{eq:npsi}, and the transport term
$\vvec{\omega}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}}\times\vvec{V}$ contributes to each
projection with a different power of $\cos\gamma$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:great-circle}
The sign of the curvature term in~\cref{eq:psidot} is fixed by a
one-line test. Take spherical gravity, a non-rotating planet, no lateral
aerodynamic force, and level flight ($\gamma = 0$) at northern latitude
$\phi > 0$ on a due-east heading ($\psi = \pi/2$). Then
$\dot\psi = (V/r)\tan\phi > 0$: with $\psi$ measured clockwise from North,
an increasing~$\psi$ turns the velocity from east toward south, which is
the equatorward bending of a great circle. The opposite sign would curve
the ground track poleward, and because the $J_2$ heading drift of
\cref{rem:j2-skip} is read off this same equation, a sign error here would
propagate into the bubble dynamics rather than cancel.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:psi-singularity}
The factor $1/\cos\gamma$ in~$\dot\psi$ is singular in vertical flight
($\gamma = \pm\pi/2$), which the corridor excludes: flight-path angles in
lifting entry are a few degrees, and at the apex of a skip $\gamma = 0$.
In interval arithmetic the factor is enclosed by evaluation of
$1/\cos[\gamma]$ provided the interval $[\gamma]$ does not contain
$\pm\pi/2$, and the same holds for $1/\cos[\phi]$ and $\tan[\phi]$ under
the polar restriction of \cref{sec:coord-rationale}.
\end{remark}


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Rotational dynamics}\label{sec:rot-dyn}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The state carries $\vvec{\omega} = (p,q_b,r_b)^\tr$, the angular velocity
of~$\mathcal{B}$ with respect to the \emph{inertial} frame, resolved in
body-frame components; this is the vector for which Euler's
equations~\cref{eq:euler} take their standard form. The quaternion
of~\cref{sec:coord-rationale} relates $\mathcal{B}$ to the \emph{rotating}
frame~$\mathcal{E}$, so its kinematics are driven by the body rate relative
to~$\mathcal{E}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:omega-rel}
  \vvec{\omega}_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{E}}
  = \vvec{\omega} - \mat{R}(\vvec{q})^\tr\,\vvec{\omega}_E ,
\end{equation}
and not by $\vvec{\omega}$ itself:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdot}
  \dot{\vvec{q}}
  = \half\,\mat{\Omega}\bigl(\vvec{\omega}_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{E}}\bigr)\,
    \vvec{q},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Omega}
  \mat{\Omega}(\vvec{a})
  = \begin{pmatrix}
      0    & -a_1 & -a_2 & -a_3\\
      a_1  &  0   &  a_3 & -a_2\\
      a_2  & -a_3 &  0   &  a_1\\
      a_3  &  a_2 & -a_1 &  0
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Euler's equations, with inertia tensor
$\mat{I} = \mat{I}(s,\eta)$ depending on recession depth and modal
amplitudes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
  \mat{I}\,\dot{\vvec{\omega}}
  = \vvec{M} - \vvec{\omega}\times(\mat{I}\,\vvec{\omega})
    - \dot{\mat{I}}_{\eta}\,\vvec{\omega},
  \qquad
  \dot{\mat{I}}_{\eta} = \sum_{i}\pd{\mat{I}}{\eta_i}\,\dot\eta_i ,
\end{equation}
where $\vvec{M} = \vvec{M}_a + \vvec{M}_{\mathrm{rcs}}$ is the total
external moment in~$\mathcal{B}$, and $\dot{\mat{I}}_{\eta}$ is the rate at
which the deformation changes the inertia at fixed mass.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:euler-mass-loss}
Only the deformation's share of $\dot{\mat{I}}$ appears in \cref{eq:euler},
and the two ways the inertia changes differ in kind. For a closed body the
angular momentum about the mass centre is $\mat{I}\,\vvec{\omega}$ when the
body axes carry no angular momentum of the deformation---the mean axes
of~\cite{canavin1977floating}---and its rate of change in inertial space is
the moment: that is where $\dot{\mat{I}}_{\eta}\,\vvec{\omega}$ comes from.
Recession is not of that kind. The mass it removes leaves from the surface
with the velocity of the surface, and takes its angular momentum with it. In
the equation of a body of variable mass the moment of the inertia variation
and the jet-damping moment appear together~\cite[Eq.~(61)]{eke2002dynamics},
and for mass that leaves from where it sat they cancel identically:
$\mat{I}$ falls, and no term in $\partial\mat{I}/\partial s$ enters.
Writing $\dot{\mat{I}}\,\vvec{\omega}$ with the total rate would count the
angular momentum of the departed mass as if it had stayed, and would spin
the vehicle up as it ablates. What the efflux can contribute is a moment of
its own, when it leaves asymmetrically and with a velocity relative to the
surface; that moment is part of $\vvec{M}$, and is enclosed with it.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:omega-transport}
\Cref{eq:omega-rel} is the one place where the choice of a rotating base
frame reaches the attitude state, and it cannot be dropped as a small
term. Omitting it leaves \cref{eq:qdot} driven by the inertial rate, which
biases the attitude by $\norm{\vvec{\omega}_E} \approx
4.18\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{deg/s}$---about $6^\circ$ over a
$1500\,\mathrm{s}$ multi-skip entry. The bias matters more than its size
suggests. It is systematic rather than bounded-uncertain, so interval
arithmetic does not absorb it: it translates the enclosure instead of
widening it, and an enclosure translated off the true attitude is no longer
an enclosure. Because the glide
balance~\cref{eq:glide-full} constrains a specific component of
$\mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{B}}\vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{B}}$, the same offset
enters the glide balance directly, and the skeleton would be extracted
about the wrong surface.
\end{remark}


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Aerodynamic force: from quaternion to
  \texorpdfstring{$(F_V,F_\gamma,F_\psi)$}
  {(FV, Fgamma, Fpsi)}}\label{sec:aero-force}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section is the bridge between the rotational and translational
dynamics.

\paragraph{Step 1: Body-frame aerodynamic force.}
Given angle of attack~$\alpha$, sideslip~$\beta$, dynamic pressure
$q_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \half\rho V^2$, and reference area~$S$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fa-body}
  \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{B}}
  = q_{\mathrm{dyn}}\,S\,
    \bigl(-C_A(\alpha,\beta,M),\;
           C_Y(\alpha,\beta,M),\;
          -C_N(\alpha,\beta,M)\bigr)^\tr.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 2: Transform to LVLH.}
Define the body-to-LVLH rotation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RLB}
  \mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{B}}({\vvec{q}},\lambda,\phi)
  \;:=\;
  \mat{R}_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{L}}^\tr(\lambda,\phi)\;
  \mat{R}(\vvec{q})
  \;=\;
  \mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}}\,\mat{R}_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{B}},
\end{equation}
so that $\vvec{v}^{\mathcal{L}} =
\mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{B}}\,\vvec{v}^{\mathcal{B}}$. Then the
aerodynamic force in~$\mathcal{L}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fa-LVLH}
  \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{L}}
  = \mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{B}}\,
    \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{B}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 3: Project onto wind axes.}
The wind-axis triad, expressed in~$\mathcal{L}$, is
\begin{align}
  \hat{V}_{\mathcal{L}} &= (\cos\gamma\cos\psi,\;
                      \cos\gamma\sin\psi,\;-\sin\gamma)^\tr,
                                                    \label{eq:Vhat}\\
  \hat{n}_\gamma &= (-\sin\gamma\cos\psi,\;
                      -\sin\gamma\sin\psi,\;
                      -\cos\gamma)^\tr, \label{eq:ngam}\\
  \hat{n}_\psi   &= \hat{V}_{\mathcal{L}} \times \hat{n}_\gamma.
                                                    \label{eq:npsi}
\end{align}
Then:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-projections}
  F_V = \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{L}}\cdot\hat{V}_{\mathcal{L}},\qquad
  F_\gamma = \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{L}}\cdot\hat{n}_\gamma,\qquad
  F_\psi = \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{L}}\cdot\hat{n}_\psi.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 4: Identification with lift, drag, bank angle.}
In the point-mass limit where the attitude is in trim,
$F_V = -D$, $F_\gamma = L\cos\sigma$, $F_\psi = L\sin\sigma$.
In the full 6-DOF formulation $D$, $L$, $\sigma$ are \emph{not independent
inputs}: they are determined by the attitude quaternion~$\vvec{q}$, the
aerodynamic coefficients, and the velocity direction $(\gamma,\psi)$
through Eqs.~(\ref{eq:Fa-body})--(\ref{eq:F-projections}).

\begin{remark}\label{rem:bank-angle}
The effective bank angle can be extracted as
$\sigma = \operatorname{atan2}(F_\psi, F_\gamma)$,
an algebraic function of
$({\vvec{q}},\lambda,\phi,\gamma,\psi,\alpha,\beta)$. Under interval
arithmetic, $\sigma$ is evaluated as an interval $\operatorname{atan2}$, a
standard operation in validated numerics~\cite{moore2009introduction}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:aoa-sideslip}
The angle of attack~$\alpha$ and sideslip~$\beta$ are
determined by the quaternion and velocity direction. Let
$\vvec{V}^{\mathcal{B}} = \mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{B}}^{\tr}\,
\vvec{V}_{\mathcal{L}}
= (V_x^{\mathcal{B}},V_y^{\mathcal{B}},V_z^{\mathcal{B}})^\tr$ be the
vehicle's velocity in body-frame components. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aoa-sideslip}
  \alpha = \operatorname{atan2}\bigl(V_z^{\mathcal{B}},
                                     V_x^{\mathcal{B}}\bigr),
  \qquad
  \beta  = \arcsin\bigl(V_y^{\mathcal{B}}/V\bigr).
\end{equation}
Both are referred to the same vector, which is the only condition that has
to be checked and the easiest one to lose: the freestream direction
is~$-\vvec{V}^{\mathcal{B}}$, and defining~$\alpha$ from the freestream
while defining~$\beta$ from the velocity offsets~$\alpha$ by~$\pi$, so that
purely axial flight $\vvec{V}^{\mathcal{B}} = (V,0,0)^\tr$ returns
$\alpha = 180^\circ$ instead of~$0$. \Cref{eq:aoa-sideslip} fixes the
velocity as the reference for both; the equivalent freestream convention is
$\alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(-V_z^{\mathcal{B}},-V_x^{\mathcal{B}})$
\emph{together with} $\beta = \arcsin(-V_y^{\mathcal{B}}/V)$. The
distinction is not cosmetic, because $\alpha$ and~$\beta$ are the arguments
of the aerodynamic coefficients in~\cref{eq:Fa-body}: a $\pi$~offset
evaluates the vehicle reversed in its own aerodynamic database, and under
interval arithmetic the resulting enclosure is wide and wrong rather than
visibly broken.
\end{remark}


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The equilibrium-glide balance}\label{sec:glide-manifold}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The equilibrium-glide condition is the balance about which the
translational dynamics organize under the thin-atmosphere singular
perturbation $\eps_{\mathrm{atm}} = H_s/R_e \to 0$. It is the set on which
the flight-path angle holds, and \cref{apx:residual} takes up in what
sense, and how weakly, trajectories are drawn to it.

Setting $\dot\gamma = 0$ in \cref{eq:gamdot}, with every term kept:
\begin{multline}\label{eq:glide-balance}
  \frac{F_\gamma}{m}
  = \Bigl(g_D - \frac{V^2}{r}\Bigr)\cos\gamma
    + g_N\sin\gamma\cos\psi
    - 2\omega_E V\cos\phi\sin\psi \\
    - \omega_E^2 r\cos\phi
      \bigl[\cos\gamma\cos\phi + \sin\gamma\cos\psi\sin\phi\bigr].
\end{multline}
For small flight-path angle, spherical gravity ($g_N = 0$,
$g_D = \mu/r^2$) and no rotation this is the classical condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glide-3dof}
  \frac{F_\gamma}{m} = g - \frac{V^2}{r},
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\quad
  \frac{L\cos\sigma}{m(g - V^2/r)} = 1
  \quad(\Lambda = 1).
\end{equation}

In the full 6-DOF formulation, $F_\gamma$ is determined by the quaternion
via Eqs.~(\ref{eq:Fa-body})--(\ref{eq:F-projections}). The balance
therefore constrains the quaternion directly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glide-full}
  \frac{1}{m}\,\bigl[\mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{B}}\,
         \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{B}}\bigr]
  \cdot\hat{n}_\gamma
  \;=\; \text{right-hand side of \cref{eq:glide-balance}},
\end{equation}
a single scalar constraint on the coupled translational and rotational state
(dimension~$13$); it defines a codimension-1 set. The skeleton of
\cref{sec:profile} is its intersection with the corresponding conditions
for the two faster motions: aerodynamic trim, on which the moment
of~\cref{eq:euler} vanishes, and quasi-static deflection, on which each
modal amplitude of~\cref{eq:modal} follows its forcing.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:manifold-attitude}
The glide balance is \emph{not} a constraint on
$\sigma$ alone---it constrains a specific component of
$\mat{R}_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{B}} \vvec{F}_a^{\mathcal{B}}$, which
depends on the full quaternion and on the aerodynamic angles
$(\alpha,\beta)$ implicitly. The ``bank angle'' that appears in the
point-mass glide condition is a derived quantity, and the balance
constrains the attitude directly.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:j2-glide}
\Cref{eq:glide-balance} retains planetary rotation and oblateness, and the
two do not enter on equal terms. Level and due east at an altitude of
$50$\,km, the terms the vertical lift has to balance are, in m/s$^2$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
 & $g - V^2/r$ & Coriolis & centrifugal & $J_2$, radial \\
 & & (equator) & (equator) & (equator to $80^\circ$) \\
\midrule
$V = 7$\,km/s & $2.04$ & $1.02$ & $0.034$ & ${+}0.015$ to ${-}0.029$ \\
$V = 5$\,km/s & $5.77$ & $0.73$ & $0.034$ & ${+}0.015$ to ${-}0.029$ \\
$V = 3$\,km/s & $8.26$ & $0.44$ & $0.034$ & ${+}0.015$ to ${-}0.029$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The Coriolis term is half of the balance at $7$\,km/s and an eighth of it
at $5$\,km/s; the centrifugal and $J_2$ terms are each under two percent of
it. The Coriolis term also depends on
heading: it vanishes for flight along a meridian and reverses sign
westbound, so with rotation retained the glide altitude is a function of
heading as well as of speed. A glide condition that kept $J_2$ and dropped
rotation would keep the smaller correction, which is why
\cref{eq:glide-balance} is written with both.

The classical form $\Lambda = L\cos\sigma/[m(g-V^2/r)] = 1$ is the reduction
under spherical gravity, no rotation and small flight-path angle. The $J_2$
correction to it acts through the $(1-3\sin^2\!\phi)$ factor of
\cref{eq:gD}: it decreases monotonically with latitude and \emph{changes
sign} partway, being ${+}\,0.16\%$ of the leading gravitational term at the
equator, zero at $\phi = \arcsin(1/\sqrt{3}) \approx 35.3^\circ$, and
${-}\,0.32\%$ at the poles. A trajectory crossing $35.3^\circ$ has the
correction act in both directions on a single pass. In interval
arithmetic it is enclosed as it stands.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:j2-skip}
What makes the $J_2$ terms worth retaining is not their size but their sign
structure over a ground track that spans many degrees of latitude. During
a multi-skip entry two $J_2$ effects act, and they have \emph{different}
sign structures---which is the substantive point, since it is what forbids
treating either as a monotone accumulation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The northward gravitational component $g_N(r,\phi)$ enters the
    heading rate $\dot\psi$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:psidot}), producing a
    latitude-dependent heading drift during each skip that is absent under
    spherical gravity. Its shape is $-\sin\phi\cos\phi$: odd in~$\phi$, so
    the sign is fixed by the hemisphere; vanishing at the equator and at
    the poles; largest at $\abs{\phi} = 45^\circ$, where it reaches
    $0.16\%$ of the leading gravitational term. Within one hemisphere the
    drift therefore keeps a single sign and \emph{does} accumulate across
    successive skips. Northward and southward skips are not symmetric. The
    Coriolis term of the same equation, $2\omega_E\sin\phi$ in level
    flight, has the same hemisphere-fixed sign and is twenty to fifty times
    larger over the range of entry speeds, so the $J_2$ drift is a
    correction to a rotation-driven one and not the leading effect.
  \item The radial component $g_D(r,\phi)$ modifies the effective
    centrifugal balance $g_D - V^2/r$ that governs the re-entry angle after
    each skip peak. Its shape is $(1-3\sin^2\!\phi)$: even in~$\phi$, so it
    acts identically in both hemispheres, and it changes sign at
    $\abs{\phi} = \arcsin(1/\sqrt{3}) \approx 35.3^\circ$, running from
    ${+}\,0.16\%$ of the leading term at the equator to ${-}\,0.32\%$ at
    the poles. A ground track confined to one side of $35.3^\circ$ thus
    accumulates this correction with a fixed sign, while one that crosses
    $35.3^\circ$ has it partially cancel between passes.
\end{enumerate}
The consequence for the certificate is a prohibition rather than an
estimate. Over $K$ skips the ground track may span a wide latitude range,
and the two effects are evaluated at a different~$\phi$ at every
re-entry; because their sign structures differ, no single worst-case
latitude bounds both at once. The enclosure may not be tightened by
assuming the corrections cancel, and need not be loosened by assuming they
reinforce: both are carried term by term. In interval arithmetic this
happens automatically: the interval evaluations of $g_D([r],[\phi])$ and
$g_N([r],[\phi])$ widen with the latitude interval, which is itself widest
during a skip, where the ground track sweeps the most latitude---and an
interval $[\phi]$ straddling $35.3^\circ$ correctly returns a $g_D$
correction of both signs rather than a spuriously signed one. Retaining
$J_2$ costs the interval evaluation nothing and removes a systematic bias of
a few tenths of a percent. It is the rotation terms, not $J_2$, that the
leading-order balance cannot do without.
\end{remark}


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Heating, and the heat shield}\label{sec:thermal}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The density of the air is a function $\rho(h)$ of the altitude~$h$ above
the reference ellipsoid; \cref{sec:density} states both. Under interval
arithmetic $\rho$ is enclosed over an interval~$[h]$ by its values at the
two ends, since it decreases with altitude.

\paragraph{Heating.}
Stagnation-point heat flux (Sutton--Graves~\cite{sutton1971general}, within
its stated validity envelope: axisymmetric blunt bodies, chemical
equilibrium, enthalpy 2.3--116.2\,MJ/kg, pressure 0.001--100\,atm), with
$Q_{\mathrm{tot}}$ the heat load it accumulates per unit area:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qdot}
  \dot Q_{\mathrm{tot}} = \dot q_s = k_Q\sqrt{\rho/R_n}\,V^3.
\end{equation}
No embedding, no recasting: $\sqrt{\rho}$ and $V^3$ are evaluated directly
as interval operations, and each is monotone in its argument. This is the
flux the corridor limits, and it is a flux to a wall at one enthalpy,
$h_{w,0}$. The wall of the model is at whatever enthalpy its own temperature
gives it, so the correlation enters the heat shield through the film
coefficient it implies,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:film}
  g_h = \frac{\dot q_s}{h_r - h_{w,0}},
  \qquad
  h_r = h_\infty + \half V^2 ,
\end{equation}
and a wall of enthalpy~$h_w$ receives $g_h\,\Omega\,(h_r - h_w)$. The factor
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blowing}
  \Omega = \frac{\Phi}{e^{\Phi}-1},
  \qquad
  \Phi = \frac{2\lambda_b\,(\dot m_c + \dot m_g)}{g_h},
\end{equation}
is the reduction of the transfer by the mass the surface blows into the
boundary layer~\cite[Eq.~(31)]{amar2008onedimensional}, with
$\lambda_b = 0.5$ in laminar flow; written in the coefficient of the blown
layer it is the $\ln(1+2\lambda_b B')/(2\lambda_b B')$
of~\cite[Eq.~(11)]{chen1999ablation}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:sutton-graves-circularity}
\Cref{eq:Qdot} is used for the corridor constraints and for the thermal
states, and deliberately not for the bubble count. Bounding the number of
passes by the integrated heat-load budget, $K \le K_{\max}(Q_{\max})$,
would make the count depend on a heating correlation, and in the unsafe
direction: an upper bound on $K$ needs a certified \emph{lower} bound on
the heat absorbed in each pass, and an equilibrium correlation does not
supply one, since heat transfer to a non-catalytic wall from a
non-equilibrium boundary layer falls below the equilibrium
value~\cite{fay1958theory}. The energy budget of \cref{eq:kmax} removes the
dependence. The count is bounded by the dissipation, which is a property
of the dynamics and of the drag enclosure alone.

What remains is the ordinary obligation to use the correlation inside its
envelope. The heat load of a pass is accumulated near its lowest point,
where the density is highest and the equilibrium assumption is at its
best; the high-altitude part of a pass, where that assumption is weakest,
contributes little. Where the corridor reaches conditions outside the
envelope, the coefficient $k_Q$ is widened to an interval or the
correlation is replaced, and the switch is enclosed as a hybrid guard.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:thermal-embedding}
The density-square-root embedding
$y = \sqrt{\rho/\rho_0}$ of the original formulation was introduced to
render the heating correlation polynomial in the states (the
Savageau--Voit recasting strategy~\cite{savageau1987recasting}). Since interval
arithmetic evaluates $\sqrt{\cdot}$ natively, this auxiliary state is
eliminated, and with it the dependency loss of carrying an extra,
constrained coordinate.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The heat shield in depth.}
Along the inward normal~$y$, with the surface at $y = s(t)$ and the back
face at $y = L$, the heat shield is a solid of bulk density~$\rho$ that
decomposes, from a virgin density~$\rho_v$ toward a char
density~$\rho_c$, into a gas that flows through it with mass flux~$j$
along~$+y$. Two things are assumed, as in the thermal-response codes this
follows~\cite{chen1999ablation}: the gas is at the temperature of the solid
it passes through, and it leaves as it is made, none being stored. The
solid is taken opaque. Mass and energy are then conserved as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slab-conservation}
  \pd{\rho}{t} + \pd{j}{y} = 0,
  \qquad
  \pd{(\rho h)}{t} + \pd{}{y}\Bigl(j\,h_g - k\,\pd{T}{y}\Bigr) = 0,
  \qquad
  \rho h = \epsilon_v\,\rho_v h_v(T) + (1-\epsilon_v)\,\rho_c h_c(T),
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon_v = (\rho-\rho_c)/(\rho_v-\rho_c)$ the fraction of the volume
still virgin. Where the first holds, the second is the temperature equation
of a charring ablator~\cite[Eq.~(1)]{chen1999ablation},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slab-temperature}
  \rho c_p\,\pd{T}{t}
  = \pd{}{y}\Bigl(k\,\pd{T}{y}\Bigr)
    + (h_g - \bar h)\,\pd{\rho}{t}
    - j\,c_{p,g}\,\pd{T}{y},
  \qquad
  \bar h = \frac{\rho_v h_v - \rho_c h_c}{\rho_v - \rho_c} ,
\end{equation}
and its two unobvious features are forced by the conservation form. The
heat capacity and the decomposition enthalpy are the two partial
derivatives of~$\rho h$, in~$T$ and in~$\rho$. And the gas, which leaves
through the surface, $j<0$, cools the solid it passes through on its way to
a hotter one. The density of a material point falls at a rate
$R(\rho,T)\ge0$ given by the kinetics of the decomposition,
$\partial_t\rho = -R$; in the three-component Arrhenius
model~\cite[Eq.~(8)]{chen1999ablation} each component has its own.

The surface is held still by Landau's
coordinate~\cite{landau1950heat}, $\eta = (y-s)/(L-s)$, in which
\begin{equation}\label{eq:landau}
  \pd{}{t}\Big|_{y} = \pd{}{t}\Big|_{\eta}
     - \frac{\dot s\,(1-\eta)}{L-s}\,\pd{}{\eta},
  \qquad
  \pd{}{y} = \frac{1}{L-s}\,\pd{}{\eta} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The surface.}
Char leaves the surface at the rate $\dot m_c = \rho_s\dot s$, and gas at
$\dot m_g = -j(s,t)$. Over a back face that is impermeable and adiabatic,
Leibniz's rule applied to \cref{eq:slab-conservation} gives what the slab
holds, per unit area:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slab-balance}
  \D{}{t}\int_s^L\rho\dd y = -(\dot m_c + \dot m_g),
  \qquad
  \D{}{t}\int_s^L\rho h\dd y = q_c - \dot m_c\,h_s - \dot m_g\,h_{g,s},
\end{equation}
with $q_c = -k\,\partial_y T|_{s}$ the heat conducted into the solid, and
$h_s$ and $h_{g,s}$ the enthalpies of the solid and of the gas at the
surface. The surface itself stores nothing, so the heat conducted in is
what arrives less what leaves:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:surface-balance}
  q_c = g_h\,\Omega\,(h_r - h_w) + \alpha_w\,q_{\mathrm{rad}}
        - \epsilon_w\,\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}\,T_w^4
        + \dot m_c\,h_s + \dot m_g\,h_{g,s}
        - (\dot m_c + \dot m_g)\,h_w .
\end{equation}
This is the surface energy balance of~\cite[Eq.~(10)]{chen1999ablation} for
equal diffusion coefficients and equal coefficients of heat and of mass
transfer; $h_w(T_w)$ is the enthalpy of the boundary-layer gas at the wall,
and $q_{\mathrm{rad}}$ the radiation of the shock layer. Put into
\cref{eq:slab-balance}, it says that the heat shield's enthalpy rises by the
net heating and falls by $(\dot m_c + \dot m_g)\,h_w$: by the enthalpy of
everything that departs, at the wall.

What removes the char is the chemistry of the surface,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recession}
  \dot s = \frac{\dot m_c}{\rho_s},
  \qquad
  \dot m_c = g_h\,B'_c\bigl(T_w,\ \dot m_g/g_h\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $B'_c$ the dimensionless rate at which char in equilibrium with the
boundary-layer gas is consumed~\cite{kendall1967multicomponent}. It is a
table of the material and of the atmosphere, and it is carried as an
enclosure. And the vehicle loses what its heat shield loses,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass-rate}
  \dot m = -\sum_k A_k\,(\dot m_c + \dot m_g)_k ,
\end{equation}
summed over stations of the surface of areas~$A_k$, each with its own slab.
\Cref{eq:state-full} carries one.

\paragraph{Cells.}
The finite-dimensional state resolves the slab on $N_T$ cells of~$\eta$,
between faces $0 = \eta_0 < \dots < \eta_{N_T} = 1$ that move with the
coordinate, at $v_i = \dot s\,(1-\eta_i)$. Write $\ell = L - s$,
$\Delta_i = \eta_i - \eta_{i-1}$, and $\delta_i$ for the distance in~$\eta$
between the centres of cells $i$ and $i+1$. Material crosses the faces
toward the surface, so each face takes its values from the cell behind it:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cells}
\begin{aligned}
  \D{}{t}\bigl(\ell\,\Delta_i\,\rho_i\bigr)
    &= -\ell\,\Delta_i\,R_i + v_i\,\rho_{i+1} - v_{i-1}\,\rho_i ,\\
  \D{}{t}\bigl(\ell\,\Delta_i\,(\rho h)_i\bigr)
    &= -(\Phi_i - \Phi_{i-1}) + v_i\,(\rho h)_{i+1} - v_{i-1}\,(\rho h)_i ,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_i = J_i\,h_g(T_{i+1}) - k\,(T_{i+1}-T_i)/(\ell\,\delta_i)$ is the
flux of \cref{eq:slab-conservation} at an interior face, the gas flux is
$J_{i-1} = J_i - \ell\,\Delta_i R_i$ from $J_{N_T} = 0$, nothing crosses the
back face, and at the surface $\Phi_0 = J_0\,h_g(T_1) + q_c$ with the wall
at the temperature of the first cell, $T_w = T_1$. Summed over the cells,
\cref{eq:cells} telescopes to \cref{eq:slab-balance} exactly, for any
$N_T$: the scheme conserves what the slab conserves. Its state is
$(T_i,\rho_i)_{i\le N_T}$ and~$s$.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:lumped-wall}
An earlier form of this appendix carried the wall as one temperature,
heated, re-radiating and conducting to a structure through a time constant,
and the recession as a fixed multiple of the heat load. The first is
\cref{eq:cells} on two cells that neither decompose nor recede, with areal
heat capacity $C_w = \rho c_p\,\ell\,\Delta_1$ and time constant
$\tau_{\mathrm{cond}} = C_w\,\ell\,\delta_1/k$: a reduction, which leaves
out the gas, the decomposition and the motion of the surface. The second is
not a reduction of anything. It makes $s$ a function of $Q_{\mathrm{tot}}$,
so that a surface far below the temperature at which its chemistry starts
recedes as fast as one above it. Recession is \cref{eq:recession}.
\end{remark}


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Modal / structural elasticity}\label{sec:modal}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The body is carried as a free beam along its axis, of width~$b(\xi)$ and
mass per unit length~$m(\xi)$ at the station~$\xi$ measured aft from the
nose. Its deflection normal to itself is expanded in the modes of free
vibration, $Z(\xi,t) = \sum_i \eta_i(t)\,W_i(\xi)$, normalized so that
$\int m\,W_iW_j\dd\xi = \delta_{ij}$. The structure lies behind the heat
shield, and what softens it is the temperature of the back face,
$T_b = T_{N_T}$ of \cref{eq:cells}. Per mode~$i$, with
temperature-dependent natural frequency:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modal}
  \ddot\eta_i + 2\zeta_i\,\omega_i(T_b)\,\dot\eta_i
  + \omega_i(T_b)^2\,\eta_i
  = Q_i,
  \qquad
  Q_i = \int f\,W_i\dd\xi ,
\end{equation}
the generalized force of the aerodynamic load~$f$ per unit length. That load
is the pressure of the air on a surface that moves, and \cref{sec:piston}
derives it. To first order in the motion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modal-forcing}
  Q_i = Q_i^{(0)} - 2\rho\,a_\infty\sum_j
        \bigl(\mathcal{C}_{ij}\,\dot\eta_j + V\,\mathcal{G}_{ij}\,\eta_j\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ and $a_\infty$ the density and the speed of sound of the
undisturbed air, $\mathcal{C}_{ij}$ and $\mathcal{G}_{ij}$ the integrals of
the mode shapes given under \cref{eq:aero-matrices}, and $Q_i^{(0)}$ the
generalized force of the pressure on the undeformed body, which belongs with
the aerodynamic coefficients of \cref{eq:Fa-body} and is enclosed with them.
The air thus adds $2\rho a_\infty\mathcal{C}$ to the damping of the
structure and $2\rho a_\infty V\mathcal{G}$ to its stiffness, each in
proportion to the density, so that both rise and fall through a pass.

The conditions under which \cref{eq:modal-forcing} holds are those of
\cref{eq:piston-validity}, and they are functions of the state, to be
checked along each atmospheric pass. At the bottom of a pass, at high angle
of attack and with strong shock interaction, they may fail, and the modal
forcing is then carried as uncertain (a widened interval).


%--------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Piston theory}\label{sec:piston}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section derives the load of \cref{eq:modal-forcing} from the equations
of gas dynamics, so that each approximation in it is in view. It follows
Lighthill~\cite{lighthill1953oscillating} and Ashley and
Zartarian~\cite{ashley1956piston}. The ratio of specific heats, their
$\gamma$, is written~$\kappa$ here, because in this appendix $\gamma$ is the
flight-path angle.

\paragraph{The slab.}
In a stream of high Mach number the shock and expansion waves lie close to
the surface. Gradients along the stream are then small beside those across
it, and the velocity disturbance is mainly across it. A plane slab of fluid
normal to the stream therefore stays plane as it is carried past the body,
and moves in its own plane as a one-dimensional unsteady flow; the relative
error is of order $1/M^2$ while $M\delta$ is bounded, $\delta$ being the
largest inclination of the surface to the
stream~\cite{lighthill1953oscillating}. Let the wall stand at $Z(\xi,t)$,
measured from the surface into the fluid. A slab carried at the speed~$U$ of
the stream, which was at the station~$\xi_0$, finds the wall at
$Z(\xi_0+Ut,\,t)$. The wall acts on it as a piston of velocity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:downwash}
  w = \pd{Z}{t} + U\,\pd{Z}{\xi} ,
\end{equation}
and the problem is reduced to the pressure on a piston that moves, with a
prescribed velocity, into gas at rest.

\paragraph{The piston.}
Take one-dimensional isentropic flow of a perfect gas along the
coordinate~$y$ across the stream, with velocity~$u$, speed of sound~$a$ and
$p\propto\rho^\kappa$. Its equations of mass and momentum are equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:riemann}
  \Bigl(\pd{}{t} + (u\pm a)\,\pd{}{y}\Bigr)
  \Bigl(u \pm \frac{2a}{\kappa-1}\Bigr) = 0 :
\end{equation}
each of the two quantities is carried unchanged along its own family of
characteristics. Ahead of the piston the gas is at rest with speed of
sound~$a_\infty$, and every characteristic of the family that runs toward
the piston starts there. So long as no shock has formed,
$u - 2a/(\kappa-1) = -2a_\infty/(\kappa-1)$ throughout. On the face of the
piston $u = w$, hence $a = a_\infty + \half(\kappa-1)\,w$, and since
$p\propto a^{2\kappa/(\kappa-1)}$ in an isentropic change,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-exact}
  \frac{p}{p_\infty}
  = \Bigl(1 + \frac{\kappa-1}{2}\,\frac{w}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{2\kappa/(\kappa-1)} .
\end{equation}
The pressure on the piston depends on its instantaneous velocity alone. That
is the property the theory is named for, and for a simple wave it holds
exactly, with no restriction on amplitude. Differentiating,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-linear}
  \D{p}{w} = \rho\,a ,
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ and~$a$ the density and the speed of sound \emph{on the face}:
the acoustic impedance of the gas, in whatever state the gas there is. About
the undisturbed state this is the small-disturbance relation
$p - p_\infty = \rho_\infty a_\infty\,w$.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:piston-local}
\Cref{eq:piston-linear} is what first-order piston theory rests on, and it
does not single out the undisturbed stream. Where the incidence of the body
is too large for \cref{eq:piston-validity} to hold of the whole motion, the
same relation applies to the perturbation that the deformation makes about
the steady flow over the undeformed body, with $\rho a$ and the speed of the
stream taken at the surface in that flow. \Cref{eq:piston-strip} and
\cref{eq:aero-matrices} are written so as to allow it. The steady flow is
then an input, and is enclosed with the aerodynamic coefficients.
\end{remark}

When the piston advances suddenly a shock runs ahead of it, and the flow is
no longer isentropic. If the shock moves at $M_s a_\infty$, conservation of
mass, momentum and energy across it give
$w/a_\infty = \tfrac{2}{\kappa+1}(M_s - 1/M_s)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-shock}
  \frac{p}{p_\infty} = 1 + \kappa\,\frac{w}{a_\infty}\,M_s ,
  \qquad
  M_s = \frac{(\kappa+1)\,w}{4a_\infty}
        + \sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{(\kappa+1)\,w}{4a_\infty}\Bigr)^{2}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The expansion.}
In powers of $w/a_\infty$ the two pressures agree further than their
derivations suggest:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-cubic}
  \frac{p}{p_\infty}
  = 1 + \kappa\,\frac{w}{a_\infty}
    + \frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)}{4}\Bigl(\frac{w}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{2}
    + c_3\Bigl(\frac{w}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{3} + \dots,
\end{equation}
with $c_3 = \kappa(\kappa+1)/12$ for the simple wave and
$c_3 = \kappa(\kappa+1)^2/32$ behind the shock.
They share their first three terms, a weak shock being isentropic to second
order in its strength, and part at the third by
$\kappa(\kappa+1)(5-3\kappa)/96$, a tenth of the coefficient for
$\kappa = 1.4$. With the simple-wave coefficient \cref{eq:piston-cubic} is
the cubic that Lighthill adopts for both cases, observing that for
$\abs{w}\le a_\infty$ it lies within about $0.06\,p_\infty$ of
each~\cite{lighthill1953oscillating}. For the simple wave the remainder is
explicit. By Taylor's theorem, for some $a_\xi$ between $a_\infty$ and the
speed of sound on the face,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-remainder}
  \frac{p}{p_\infty} - \Bigl[\,1 + \dots + \frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)}{12}
      \Bigl(\frac{w}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{3}\Bigr]
  = \frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)(3-\kappa)}{96}\,
    \Bigl(\frac{w}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{4}
    \Bigl(\frac{a_\xi}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{\frac{2\kappa}{\kappa-1}-4} ,
\end{equation}
which is positive for $1<\kappa<3$: the cubic lies below the simple wave in
compression and in expansion alike. For $\kappa = 1.4$ and
$\abs{w}\le a_\infty$ the right-hand side is at most $0.097$; the gap
itself is largest at $w = a_\infty$, where it is $0.063$. Only the
first-order term enters \cref{eq:modal-forcing}. The higher terms are what
the enclosure of the forcing has to cover, and \cref{eq:piston-remainder} is
the form in which it can.

\paragraph{What the first-order relation leaves out.}
The slab is an asymptotic picture, and there is one case in which it can be
set against an exact solution. Let the wall be a wave of arbitrary profile
travelling along the stream, $Z = G(k\xi - \omega t)$, and let the flow be
the linearized potential flow about a uniform stream. Put
$M_r = (U - \omega/k)/a_\infty$, the Mach number of the stream relative to
the wave. For $M_r > 1$ the solution that radiates away from the wall gives,
on the wall,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-wavy}
  p - p_\infty = \rho_\infty a_\infty\,w\;\frac{M_r}{\sqrt{M_r^2-1}} ,
  \qquad
  0 \;<\; \frac{M_r}{\sqrt{M_r^2-1}} - 1 \;\le\; \frac{1}{2\,(M_r^2-1)} ,
\end{equation}
with $w$ the piston velocity of \cref{eq:downwash}. For a wall at rest
$M_r = M$, and this is Ackeret's formula,
$C_p = 2\,\partial_\xi Z/\sqrt{M^2-1}$. First-order piston theory is its
limit as $M_r\to\infty$, and the relative error of the limit is the excess
displayed: two percent at $M_r = 5$. For a wave that runs upstream at the
speed~$c$, $M_r^2 = M^2 + 2(c/U)M^2 + (c/U)^2M^2$, whose three terms are the
three quantities of which Ashley and Zartarian require one to be
large~\cite{ashley1956piston}. For a wave carried downstream at nearly the
speed of the stream $\abs{M_r} < 1$, the disturbance does not radiate, and
the relation fails however large~$M$ is. The bending waves of a vehicle are
slow beside the stream, and for them $M_r \approx M$.

Beyond first order, the orders of what is left out are taken from the
sources and are not derived here. With $\delta$ now the larger of the
thickness ratio and the amplitude of the motion over the chord,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-validity}
  M^2 \gg 1, \qquad \abs{w} < a_\infty, \qquad
  C_p - C_p^{\mathrm{piston}}
  = \mathcal{O}\bigl(\delta/M^{3}\bigr) + \mathcal{O}\bigl(\delta^{2}/M^{2}\bigr) .
\end{equation}
The second is Lighthill's condition on the speed of the surface, under which
the pressure stays between $0.2$ and $3.5$ times
$p_\infty$~\cite{lighthill1953oscillating}; the third is the error of the
expansion carried to second order~\cite{ashley1956piston}, and for a wall at
rest its first term is the excess of \cref{eq:piston-wavy}. The third-order
term of \cref{eq:piston-cubic} lies outside that analysis, and Ashley and
Zartarian call its justification excellent but empirical.

\paragraph{The load on a strip.}
A thin surface has two faces, and a motion that advances one of them into
the stream withdraws the other. If the thickness gives both the piston
velocity~$w_s$ and the motion gives them $\pm w_i$, the cubic of
\cref{eq:piston-cubic} leaves, for the pressure on the lower face less that
on the upper~\cite[Eq.~(8)]{lighthill1953oscillating},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-load}
  \frac{\Delta p}{p_\infty}
  = \Bigl[\,2\kappa + \kappa(\kappa+1)\frac{w_s}{a_\infty}
      + \frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)}{2}\Bigl(\frac{w_s}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]
    \frac{w_i}{a_\infty}
    + \frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)}{6}\Bigl(\frac{w_i}{a_\infty}\Bigr)^{3} ,
\end{equation}
with no term in~$w_i^2$: at second order the thickness changes the
coefficient of the motion and does nothing else. To first order
$\Delta p = 2\rho_\infty a_\infty\,w_i$. As a check on the sign and on the
factor of two, a flat strip at incidence~$\alpha$ presents the normal
velocity $V\sin\alpha$ to the stream, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-flat}
  C_N = \frac{2\rho_\infty a_\infty\,V\sin\alpha}{\half\rho_\infty V^2}
      = \frac{4\sin\alpha}{M} ,
\end{equation}
the form at high Mach number of the linear result
$4\alpha/\sqrt{M^2-1}$, which the factor of \cref{eq:piston-wavy} restores.
No other use is made of it; the coefficients of the body are enclosed and
are not computed from piston theory.

For the beam of \cref{sec:modal}, with deflection
$Z = \sum_j\eta_j(t)\,W_j(\xi)$, the force per unit length along the
deflection is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:piston-strip}
  f = -\,b\,z_a\Bigl(\pd{Z}{t} + U\,\pd{Z}{\xi}\Bigr) ,
\end{equation}
opposing the piston velocity, where $z_a$ is the sum of the acoustic
impedances~$\rho a$ on the two faces and $U$ the speed of the stream along
the strip. Projected on the modes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aero-matrices}
  \int f\,W_i\dd\xi
  = -\sum_j\bigl(C_{ij}\,\dot\eta_j + G_{ij}\,\eta_j\bigr),
  \qquad
  C_{ij} = \int b\,z_a\,W_iW_j\dd\xi ,
  \quad
  G_{ij} = \int b\,z_a\,U\,W_i\,W_j'\dd\xi .
\end{equation}
Under a uniform stream $z_a = 2\rho_\infty a_\infty$ and $U = V$, so that
$C = 2\rho_\infty a_\infty\,\mathcal{C}$ and
$G = 2\rho_\infty a_\infty V\,\mathcal{G}$ with
$\mathcal{C}_{ij} = \int b\,W_iW_j\dd\xi$ and
$\mathcal{G}_{ij} = \int b\,W_iW_j'\dd\xi$: the integrals of
\cref{eq:modal-forcing}.

Two things follow from the form of these matrices. $C$ is a Gram matrix. The
power of the damping term is
$-\int b\,z_a\,(\partial_t Z)^2\dd\xi \le 0$, so it can only dissipate; it
is the radiation of sound by a moving wall~\cite{lighthill1953oscillating}.
$G$ is not symmetric. Integrating by parts,
\[
  G_{ij} + G_{ji}
  = \bigl[\,b\,z_a\,U\,W_iW_j\,\bigr]_{\mathrm{ends}}
    - \int (b\,z_a\,U)'\,W_iW_j\dd\xi ,
\]
so that its symmetric part is a boundary term and a term in the gradient of
the stream, and what remains is antisymmetric. An antisymmetric stiffness
has no potential. It moves energy between modes, and it is through it that
the air can feed an oscillation instead of damping it. The mechanism of
flutter is therefore in the equations. No margin against it is claimed.

\subsection{Altitude, and the density model}\label{sec:density}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The atmosphere lies on the figure of the planet and not on a sphere, so the
altitude the density is a function of is measured from the reference
ellipsoid. With $a = R_e$ the equatorial radius and $f$ the flattening, the
ellipsoid meets the radius at the geocentric latitude~$\phi$ at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ellipsoid}
  R_\oplus(\phi) = \frac{a\,(1-f)}{\sqrt{1 - f(2-f)\cos^2\phi}} ,
\end{equation}
and the altitude is taken along the radius,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:altitude}
  h = r - R_\oplus(\phi) .
\end{equation}
For the Earth, $a = 6378137$\,m and $1/f = 298.257223563$~\cite{nima2000department}.
This is not a refinement of $r - R_e$. At latitude $27^\circ$ the ellipsoid
is $4.4$\,km inside the equatorial radius, most of a scale height, and the
altitude enters the drag through an exponential. The oblateness of the
figure matters to an entry far more than the oblateness of the field: the
$J_2$ terms of \cref{eq:gD} are kept for a correction of a few tenths of a
percent, and a spherical altitude would be wrong by a factor approaching
two in the density.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:geodetic-altitude}
The altitude of \cref{eq:altitude} is not the geodetic altitude~$h_g$, which
is measured along the normal to the ellipsoid, at the geodetic
latitude~$\varphi$. The difference has no term of first order in the
flattening,
\[
  h - h_g = \tfrac12\,f^2\,\frac{a\,h_g}{a+h_g}\,\sin^2 2\varphi
            + \mathcal{O}(f^3) ,
\]
which is under $0.73$\,m below $130$\,km, a relative $10^{-4}$ of the
density at most. It is carried as stated: a density given in geodetic
altitude is evaluated at~$h$. What does not survive the confusion is
direction. The normal and the radius differ by $f\sin 2\varphi$ to first
order, up to $0.19^\circ$, so a flight-path angle referred to the geodetic
horizon differs by as much from the $\gamma$ of \cref{eq:state-full}, which
is referred to the geocentric one: for the entry of \cref{fig:skip}, by
$0.15^\circ$ in an angle of $5.7^\circ$.
\end{remark}

The density itself is defined in two parts, of different standing. Below
$86$\,km it is the US Standard Atmosphere~1976~\cite{noaa1976standard}, as
defined: seven layers of the geopotential altitude $H = r_0h/(r_0+h)$, in
each of which the molecular-scale temperature $T_M = T_b + L\,(H - H_b)$ is
linear, so that hydrostatic equilibrium of a perfect gas gives the pressure
in closed form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standard-layer}
  p = p_b\Bigl(\frac{T_b}{T_M}\Bigr)^{g_0M_0/(R^*L)}
  \ \ (L\neq0),
  \qquad
  p = p_b\exp\Bigl(-\frac{g_0M_0\,(H-H_b)}{R^*\,T_b}\Bigr)
  \ \ (L=0),
  \qquad
  \rho = \frac{p\,M_0}{R^*\,T_M} .
\end{equation}
Nothing in it is fitted, and the base values $(T_b,p_b)$ of each layer
follow from the layer below. Above $86$\,km the standard has no closed
form. There the density is a fit: a monotone piecewise cubic in $\ln\rho$
through $69$ knots of the NRLMSISE-00 model~\cite{picone2002nrlmsise} at
moderate solar activity, which stays within $0.08\%$ of that model. The two
are blended over $80$--$86$\,km, and they do not meet there: at $86$\,km
NRLMSISE-00 is $6\%$ denser than the standard, and its successor
NRLMSIS~2.0~\cite{emmert2021nrlmsis} is $11\%$ thinner.

That settles the functional form, and it is the one interval evaluation
needs: the density is decreasing in~$h$, so its enclosure over an interval
of altitudes is its values at the two ends. What is not settled is the
envelope,
\[
  \rho_{\mathrm{lower}}(h) \le \rho(h) \le \rho_{\mathrm{upper}}(h) ,
\]
valid across the altitude range of the corridor. Its width is not a fitting
error. It is the distance between a model atmosphere and the air a vehicle
meets, which on the lunar return of Artemis~I was about ten percent thinner
than the standard~\cite{rea2024orion}, and fixing it with a stated
provenance is the first thing \cref{obj:embedding} has to do. The width
$\rho_{\mathrm{upper}} - \rho_{\mathrm{lower}}$ at each altitude is the
primary driver of the aerodynamic interval widths.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule

\bottomrule

\smallskip\noindent
The spherical-LVLH choice places the dependency loss in the trigonometric
factors of the dynamics, each a function of a single state that encloses
tightly. The Cartesian choice places it in the corridor and the glide
balance, which become nonlinear functions of several states and are
evaluated at every contraction. Neither choice touches the wrapping effect
proper, which is a property of how the propagated set is represented and is
not reduced by refining the time step: re-boxed at every step, a rotation
inflates a box by a factor that tends to $e^{2\pi}$ per revolution as the
step vanishes, and the phugoid of \cref{apx:residual} is such a rotation.
For the profile decomposition---which is organized around the corridor and
the glide---the spherical choice is the better fit, at the price of the
polar restriction of \cref{sec:coord-rationale}.
