\begin{abstract}
The reachable set of a nonlinear control system can be embedded in a space
of occupation measures, where the dynamics become a linear constraint and
the reachable set is the union of the supports of the admissible terminal
measures. This research asks what that embedding yields in a singular
limit, for a vector field known only as a set-valued map enclosed in
validated interval arithmetic. On the state space nothing is lost---the
family is tight---but its weak limit forgets every event that lasts a
vanishing time, and a reachable set is a support, not an integral: the
limit satisfies the Liouville equation of the limit dynamics only up to a
defect. The target theorem identifies that defect. The loss is read on the
energy dissipation along the rescaled time axis, where Lions'
concentration-compactness alternative applies as it stands, and it
organizes into a profile decomposition: a limit system in a few slow
coordinates, one boundary-layer bubble per concentrating event, entering as
a jump, and a remainder. The statement aimed at holds at fixed~$\eps$: the
reachable set lies in that of the limit hybrid system, thickened by a ball
whose radius is an explicit function of~$\eps$. Compactness gives the
structure and no constants. Those are to come from direct estimates---the
energy identity, a matching estimate at each pass, and averaging over the
oscillations that do not concentrate, where frequencies that cross are the
critical difficulty---and are required in closed form, in the physical
constraint set and the aerodynamic interval widths. Hypersonic entry
supplies the instance: a six-degree-of-freedom vehicle whose
thin-atmosphere limit furnishes the singular perturbation, whose
exo-atmospheric arcs and equilibrium glide furnish the limit system, and
whose atmospheric passes furnish the bubbles. The aim is a bound whose
dependence on the data is explicit: valid uniformly over the entire
admissible family, in the minimal physical state space, and re-evaluated
rather than re-solved when the vehicle or the corridor changes. The central
theorem is open, and this document claims no result: it states the problem,
the statement aimed at, and the order in which it is to be attacked.
\end{abstract}

\section{The analytical problem}
%==========================================================================
The object to be bounded is the reachable set of an atmospheric entry
vehicle---every state it can be driven to under admissible control---which
is what a safety analysis needs enclosed. The flight-mechanics
specifics are deferred: \cref{sec:application} states why entry is the
right instance and what its state space looks like. What matters first is
that the
problem is an analytical one before it is an aerospace one, and that the
existing methods fall short of it on two independent counts: the dimension
of the state, taken up with the application in \cref{sec:application}, and
the provenance of the guarantee, in \cref{sec:provenance}.

\subsection{Reachable sets as occupation measures}\label{sec:embedding}
Embed the flight dynamics in a space of measures. Along a trajectory of
$\dot x = f(x,c,t)$, with the state $x$ in the corridor $\calX$ and the
control $c$ in $\calU$, the time spent in each set defines an occupation
measure $\mu$ on $[0,T]\times\calX\times\calU$, and the nonlinear flight
mechanics become \emph{linear}: the Liouville equation is a linear
constraint tying $\mu$ to an initial measure and to a terminal measure
$\mu_T$, and the reachable set at time~$T$ is the union of
$\operatorname{supp}\mu_T$ over the measures that satisfy it. No single
measure bounds it. The outer bound comes from the dual: a pair of
functions $(\cert,\certT)$, the first non-decreasing along every admissible
trajectory and at least one on the initial set, the second majorizing it at
time~$T$, encloses the reachable set in the superlevel set
$\{\certT\ge 1\}$. This is the occupation-measure program of Lasserre,
Henrion and
collaborators~\cite{lasserre2008nonlinear,vinter1993convex}, developed for
regions of attraction and invariant
sets~\cite{henrion2014convex,korda2014convex} and, for hybrid systems, for
reachable sets and optimal control, where a measure on each guard carries
the mass from one mode into the next~\cite{shia2014convex,zhao2020optimal}.
The embedding buys linearity at the price of a relaxation, and the price is
known: a measure that satisfies the Liouville constraint is generated by a
family of trajectories of the \emph{convexified} inclusion
$\dot x\in\operatorname{conv} f(x,\calU,t)$, not by a single trajectory of
the original system~\cite[Lemma~3]{henrion2014convex}. The relaxed
reachable set contains the true one, so an outer bound on the first is an
outer bound on the second, which is the direction needed here.

The same relaxation admits the interval envelope. Because the true
aerodynamics are transcendental and known only to within an envelope, they
are enclosed in intervals, and a vector field selected from an interval is
a set-valued map; the resulting differential inclusion is the object the
measure must describe, not the nominal
field~\cite{filippov1988differential,aubin1984differential}. Written with
the selection as an additional box-valued input, the inclusion is a control
system with a bounded disturbance, and at fixed $\eps$ its Liouville
constraint is the standard one. What is not standard is carrying the outer
approximation through the singular limit of \cref{sec:profile}, uniformly
over the selections. Separating time scales is itself a standard device of
flight mechanics~\cite{naidu2001singular}, and the classical reduction,
which sets $\eps=0$, requires the fast motion to settle. Occupation-measure
theory for inclusions that does not require it already exists, and it is
the nearest prior art: it defines an occupational invariant measure without
assuming solution uniqueness, which is the same move made here, and lets
the slow dynamics be driven by the invariant measures of the
fast~\cite{artstein1999invariant}. What it assumes is
worth stating plainly, because it locates where the entry problem leaves
it. The linear growth condition on the field serves only to exclude
finite-time blow-up, and is automatic on a bounded corridor. The
singular-perturbation theorem, however, assumes a uniform bound on the
fast state along the whole
$\eps$-family~\cite{artstein1999invariant,artstein2000value}, and the
natural fast state of entry is the altitude measured in scale heights: on a
corridor of fixed altitude extent it ranges over an interval of length
proportional to $1/\eps$, the aerodynamic terms grow exponentially in it,
and a skip that rises through a fixed altitude carries it out of every
bounded set. The hypothesis holds for motion that stays within a bounded
number of scale heights of the glide, and it fails exactly where a pass
concentrates; \cref{sec:loss} says which is which.

\begin{openitem}{Interval embedding, uniform through the singular limit}
\label{obj:embedding}
\emph{Question.} What do the occupation measures of the interval inclusion
$\dot x \in \calF_\eps(x,c,t)$ converge to as $\eps\to 0$, and what linear
constraint does the limit satisfy?

\emph{To establish.} At fixed $\eps$ the inclusion is a control system with
a box-valued disturbance and its Liouville constraint is standard. So are
the bounds that hold uniformly in $\eps$: on mass, on support and, through
the energy balance, on dissipation. Two things are not.
The first is a certified upper and lower envelope of the atmospheric
density, which fixes $\calF_\eps$ concretely. The
second is the limit of the constraint itself. A weak-$*$ limit of the
measures satisfies the Liouville equation of the limit flows only up to a
defect, carried where the dissipation concentrates. The claim to be proved
is that this defect is a jump term, on a measure supported on the graph of
the pass map, so that the limit satisfies the Liouville equation of the
hybrid system of \cref{sec:bound}, in the form with guard
measures~\cite{shia2014convex,zhao2020optimal},
uniformly over the selections. Where nothing concentrates the limit is of
the kind the existing theory describes, with the oscillation about the
glide represented by an invariant
measure~\cite{artstein1999invariant}.

\emph{Success.} The limit constraint stated for the full inclusion, with
each hypothesis either verified on the entry corridor or named as an
assumption; and its dual, a pair $(\cert,\certT)$ non-decreasing along the
flows and across the jumps of the limit system, as the form in which the
outer bound of \cref{sec:embedding} survives the limit.
\end{openitem}

\subsection{The provenance of the guarantee}\label{sec:provenance}
A polynomial barrier whose coefficients come from an interior-point solver
has analytical \emph{form} but inherits the solver's
accuracy~\cite{prajna2007framework}, and the documented failure modes of
numerical semidefinite solvers include the loss of strict feasibility on
which the repair schemes depend~\cite{roux2018validating}. A solver's
output can be made into a certificate: rounded to rational coefficients and
re-verified exactly, it is a proof~\cite{peyrl2008computing}. The obstacle
is scale, not principle. The rounding needs a strictly feasible Gram
matrix, which a tight bound does not have, and exact rational certificates
carry a documented bit-complexity whose exponent grows with the number of
variables~\cite{kaltofen2012exact,magron2021exact}---a cost the
thirteen-state field of \cref{sec:application} does not invite as a main
line.

The program therefore fixes an order of evidence. The certificate itself is
a closed-form bound: the constants of \cref{sec:profile}, derived from the
decomposition and evaluated over the corridor in validated interval
arithmetic. Behind it are to stand symbolic checks of the derivations
those constants rest on, made against the same definition of the dynamics
that is evaluated numerically. A moment-sum-of-squares
computation on a reduced model is the third tier: an independent
cross-check, to which the exact-certification machinery above belongs, and
never the source of the guarantee.

Interval arithmetic is the setting in which a guarantee has meant, for
sixty years, an enclosure rather than an approximation. It is rigorous in
floating point: every operation is rounded outward, so the computed
interval contains the exact result, and nothing is gained for soundness by
exact rational arithmetic, which cannot in any case evaluate an exponential
or a sine~\cite[Ch.~3]{moore2009introduction}. Two distinct losses have to
be managed, and they are often run together. The first is the
\emph{dependency problem}. Interval arithmetic is inclusion
isotonic---the property that makes an enclosure valid at all---but not
every law of real arithmetic survives the move to intervals: distributivity
weakens to subdistributivity, and repeated occurrences of a variable are
treated as independent~\cite[Chs.~4--5]{moore2009introduction}. The second
is the \emph{wrapping effect}: the image of a box under a flow is not a
box, and re-enclosing it at every step admits points that no trajectory
reaches~\cite[Ch.~10]{moore2009introduction}. Refining the step does not
help. Under interval Euler steps of size~$h$, re-boxed at each, a rigid
rotation inflates a box by $(1+h)^{2\pi/h}$ per revolution, which
\emph{increases} to $e^{2\pi}\approx 535$ as $h\to 0$; what controls
wrapping is the
representation of the set---Lohner's QR factorization, zonotopes, Taylor
models~\cite{nedialkov1999validated,makino2003taylor}. Applied interval
analysis supplies the machinery the evaluation rests on, and it is
deliberately conservative: certified set inversion, contractors, and
validated integration~\cite{jaulin2001applied}.

\begin{openitem}{Validated evaluation of the certificate}
\label{obj:pipeline}
\emph{Question.} Can the closed-form constants, the pass enclosures they do
not cover, and the reachable set of the limit system of \cref{sec:bound} be
evaluated over the whole corridor with every rounding error accounted for?

\emph{To establish.} The evaluation, as a computer-assisted step of the
proof: the transcendental field and its Jacobian enclosed with outward
rounding, the corridor constraints contracted, and the passes integrated
with validated error bounds. The limit system is itself a reachability
problem, in the few slow coordinates and over every bank history: it is to
be enclosed by comparison bounds in closed form where they exist, and by
validated set propagation where they do not.

\emph{Success.} Every inequality the certificate asserts is discharged by a
computation that returns either a proof or an explicit failure to decide at
the working precision, and the dynamics it evaluates are the dynamics that
are checked symbolically.
\end{openitem}

\section{The singular limit and its profile decomposition}
\label{sec:profile}
%==========================================================================
This section carries the new content. The instance supplies a small
parameter---in the entry problem the thin-atmosphere ratio
$\eps_{\mathrm{atm}} = H_s/R_e \to 0$, where $H_s$ is the atmospheric
scale height and $R_e$ the planetary radius, written $\eps$ where the other
small parameters are not in play---and the question is what the
occupation-measure family does in that limit.

\subsection{Loss of compactness along the time axis and the defect measure}
\label{sec:loss}
On the corridor nothing is lost in the usual sense. The set
$[0,T]\times\calX\times\calU$ is compact, so the occupation measures---which
live in the cone $\Mplus([0,T]\times\calX\times\calU)$ of nonnegative
Radon measures, topologized weakly-$*$ as the dual of
$C([0,T]\times\calX\times\calU)$---form a tight family and converge along
subsequences. The limit is nevertheless the wrong object, because it
forgets the atmospheric passes (\cref{fig:skip}). How much it forgets
depends on which limit is taken, and that has to be said first.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/skip-entry.pdf}
  \caption{A skip entry at the physical scale height, by way of example:
    the lunar return of Artemis~I, simulated from the published entry
    state~\cite{rea2024orion}. Above, altitude. Below, the drag power per
    unit mass~$P$, the
    rate at which mechanical energy is dissipated. The shaded intervals are
    the two atmospheric passes, on which $P$ exceeds a tenth of its peak;
    between them the vehicle lofts and dissipates little. An occupation
    measure weighs every interval by its length. What distinguishes one
    trajectory from another is decided in the shaded ones, and in the limit
    they have no length.}
  \label{fig:skip}
\end{figure}

A pass entered at a flight-path angle $\gamma_e$ turns the velocity through
about $2\gamma_e$ and sheds a share of the kinetic energy of order
$\gamma_e/(L/D)$, since lift cannot be had without
drag. It crosses a scale height in a time
$H_s/(V\gamma_e)$, of order $\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}/\gamma_e$ against the slow
time, and it levels out where the lift is $V^2\gamma_e^2/(2H_s)$, of order
$\gamma_e^2/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ against gravity.
The entry angle therefore enters through
$\gamma_e/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$---as in the classical Chapman--Loh
variables, it is $\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$ and not $\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$
that sets the scale~\cite{chapman1959approximate,loh1968reentry}---and two
limits have to be told apart. With $\gamma_e/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$
held fixed, a pass lasts a time of order $\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$, sheds
an energy of the same order, and loads the vehicle by a fixed multiple of
what the glide does. This is the limit of a corridor whose dynamic-pressure
and load limits are held fixed, and in it nothing concentrates: the passes
are many and slight, the troughs of an oscillation about the glide. With
$\gamma_e$ held fixed, a pass lasts a time of order $\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$
and sheds a fixed share of the energy, under loads that grow like
$1/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$. It carries occupation mass of that order, and none
in the limit, while its share of what distinguishes one trajectory from
another---the energy dissipated, the turn of the velocity vector---does not
vanish with it. The physical case lies between
the two. $\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}\approx 0.034$ is about two degrees and
entry angles are a few degrees, so a pass need be neither short against the
arc that follows it nor distinct in scale from the oscillation about the
glide. The statement aimed at is
therefore one at fixed $\eps$, with a threshold that decides which passes
are resolved one by one, and the two limits are the ends it has to cover.

A reachable set is a support, not an integral, and what has no mass still
counts. That alone does not set this problem apart from the limit theory of
singularly perturbed control, whose value functions already carry a
terminal cost on the slow state~\cite{artstein2000value}. What sets it
apart is the hypothesis under which that theory works: the fast state, and
the trajectories that approximate an optimum, stay in a bounded set, and a
pass that concentrates is what that excludes. The loss has to be read where
it occurs, along the fast-time axis
$\tau = t/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$, on which the horizon
$[0,T/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}]$ exhausts the half-line and translations
act non-compactly.

Between passes the vehicle is outside the sensible atmosphere, on an arc
governed by gravity alone; once captured, it stays near the
equilibrium-glide balance $\Lambda = 1$. In the 3-DOF (point-mass)
approximation this is the scalar condition
$L\cos\sigma/[m(g - V^2/r)] = 1$; in the full 6-DOF formulation the lift
component $L\cos\sigma$ is not an independent input but is determined by
the attitude quaternion through the body-to-LVLH
rotation, so the balance is
a single scalar constraint on the 13-dimensional translational-rotational
state, and the slow set is its intersection with the trim and
quasi-static-deflection conditions of the two faster motions---one
constraint set for each. The balance is taken with planetary rotation and
oblate gravity retained, and the two do not enter
on equal terms: flying east at entry speed the Coriolis term is tens of
percent of the net acceleration $g - V^2/r$ that the lift has to balance,
while the $J_2$ terms are a percent of it at most. The
scalar $\Lambda = 1$ is the reduction under spherical gravity, no rotation
and small flight-path angle.

The loss is read on a \emph{defect measure}, and the object has to be chosen
so that the passes carry mass. The part of the occupation-measure limit
that lies off the glide cannot serve, since the passes have none. The
energy dissipation can. Along every trajectory of the inclusion the
specific mechanical energy in the rotating frame,
$\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 V^2 + \Phi_g - \tfrac12\omega_E^2 r^2\cos^2\phi$,
changes at the rate $\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -P$, where $P = DV/m \ge 0$ is the
drag power: neither the lift nor the Coriolis force does
work. The total dissipation
$\int_0^T P\dd t \le \mathcal{E}_{\max} - \mathcal{E}_{\min}$ is therefore
bounded uniformly over controls, over selections of the interval field,
and over $\eps$. Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:defect}
  \nu_\eps(\tau)
  \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{\eps}\;P\bigl(\sqrt{\eps}\,\tau\bigr)}
             {\int_0^T P\dd t}
\end{equation}
be the dissipation density on the fast-time axis, normalized to unit mass
and extended by zero. It is proposed as the defect measure. Where it
concentrates is where the limit of the occupation measures can stop
satisfying the Liouville equation of the limit flow, and the mass it puts
there should bound the size of the failure, because the aerodynamic impulse
over any interval is bounded by the energy dissipated in it. Along any
sequence $\eps_n\to 0$, with a trajectory chosen for each, the $\nu_\eps$
are nonnegative densities of fixed mass on the line, which is exactly the
hypothesis of Lions' concentration-compactness
lemma~\cite[Lemma~I.1]{lions1984concentration1}, and here all three of its
alternatives mean something:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \emph{vanishing}---no window of fixed fast-time width holds a fixed
    share of the dissipation: the glide and the oscillation about it, on
    which the drag is bounded and such a window holds a share of order
    $\sqrt{\eps}$;
  \item \emph{compactness}, up to translation---one pass carries the
    dissipation;
  \item \emph{dichotomy}---the dissipation splits between pieces whose
    fast-time separation diverges: two passes a positive time apart, or a
    pass followed by a glide.
\end{itemize}
Which of the three occurs is decided by the scaling above. With the loads
held fixed the drag power is bounded and every sequence vanishes.
Concentration needs an entry angle that stays large against $\sqrt{\eps}$.
Then translations along the fast-time axis separate the passes, and it is
the dilation half of the principle---the limit-case
modification~\cite{lions1985limit1}---that resolves each one at its own
width, since a pass shorter than $\sqrt{\eps}$ is a Dirac mass at that
scale. The trichotomy is Lions' lemma applied as stated. That the vanishing
part is the glide and the concentrated pieces are the passes is asymptotics
still to be argued, from the inner and outer expansions of the classical
theory~\cite{shi1971skip}.

Measures that keep track of concentration are not new to this subject. An
unbounded control that enters the dynamics affinely is relaxed to a measure
in time whose atoms are jumps of the state, and oscillation and
concentration of a control sequence have been carried together by the
measures of DiPerna and
Majda~\cite[and the references there]{claeys2017semidefinite}. There the
concentration is put in by the control. Here the control is bounded and the
limit produces it, through a force that depends on the state, so what a
jump does is the solution of an inner problem; bounding the impulse alone
gives a limit that is sound and far too large.

\subsection{Skeleton, bubbles, residual}
Rather than restore compactness, the program extracts the profile
decomposition that iterating the dichotomy hands it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{skeleton}, low-dimensional---what survives the limit: the
    exo-atmospheric arcs between passes, which are conics up to the
    oblateness correction, followed by the equilibrium glide, a graph over
    $(r,V)$ in the corridor subspace once the balance is solved for the
    vertical lift fraction. For the glide a rigorous characterization with
    quantitative accuracy estimates already
    exists~\cite{lu2006asymptotic}, and it treats the glide as a
    \emph{regular} perturbation: the exact solution is not attracted to the
    zeroth-order one, a point that returns in \cref{sec:bound};

  \item one \emph{boundary-layer bubble} per atmospheric pass, the analogue
    of a concentrating profile: the pass rescaled to its own width. Its net
    effect on the slow coordinates is a jump. The exit states that the bank
    history and the selections can produce from one entry state form a
    compact set, and the map that assigns it, the \emph{pass map}~$J$, is
    the object to be enclosed. Orthogonality is read off the scale-and-core
    separation condition that governs concentrating profiles
    generally~\cite{gerard1998description}. The decomposition of skip entry
    into an aerodynamic inner region and a gravitational outer one is
    classical~\cite{shi1971skip,chapman1959approximate}, and it has been
    carried to second order and to three
    dimensions~\cite{kuo1995improved}. Those are expansions of one
    trajectory, with the error assessed by example. What is not classical
    is a statement uniform over a family of trajectories, with an explicit
    remainder at fixed $\eps$;

  \item a \emph{residual}: what the finitely many profiles do not capture.
    In a profile decomposition the remainder is not compact and is not
    small in the conserved norm; it is small in a weaker one, and only as
    the number of extracted profiles
    grows~\cite{bahouri1999high,solimini1995note}. Here the weaker norm is
    the largest share of dissipation held by any fast-time window: on a
    glide the residual carries most of the dissipation, and almost none of
    it in any one window. The quantitative bound the program needs is a
    different statement, a distance in state space, and it is taken up in
    \cref{sec:bound}.
\end{itemize}

The two kinds of statement are kept apart, because they come from different
arguments. A decomposition obtained by compactness is a statement about
subsequences, and what it gives uniformly it gives by contradiction: it
says which pieces can occur, and that nothing else can, and it produces no
constant. The wave-equation theory is explicit about this. The uniform
bound that follows from its decomposition comes with a constant its authors
say they cannot describe~\cite[Corollary~2]{bahouri1999high}. Here
compactness is asked only for the structure. The constants come from direct
estimates: the energy identity, the bound it gives on the aerodynamic
impulse, a matching estimate at each pass, and averaging over the
oscillations that do not concentrate. Each of those is an inequality with a
constant that can be written down, and writing them down is
\cref{obj:constants}.

The number of bubbles is bounded \emph{a~priori}, and the bound comes from
the same monotone quantity. A pass that dissipates at least $\delta$ of
specific energy can occur at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kmax}
  K_\delta \;\le\; \frac{\mathcal{E}_{\max}-\mathcal{E}_{\min}}{\delta}
\end{equation}
times, for every control and every selection of the interval field. Passes
below the threshold are left to the oscillation about the glide and to the
residual, which is the device of the profile-decomposition literature: only
finitely many profiles exceed any given size. The integrated heat-load
budget gives the count its physical reading---a trajectory can only afford
so many skips~\cite{shi1971skip,eggers1958comparative}---but it routes
through a stagnation-point heating correlation, and the energy budget does
not.

Enclosing a pass is where the wrapping effect has to be met. For linear
systems it can be avoided altogether: support-function methods propagate
each direction independently, so successive over-approximations do not
compound, and what remains is a discretization error of first order in the
time step~\cite{leguernic2010reachability}. Nothing comparable is available
for a nonlinear pass.

\begin{openitem}{Enclosures of the pass maps}
\label{obj:passmaps}
\emph{Question.} Can the net effect of one atmospheric pass on the slow
coordinates be enclosed tightly enough that a sequence of $K_\delta$
passes does not exhaust the corridor?

\emph{To prove and compute.} Wrapping in a pass has a specific source.
Trajectories of a set enter the layer at different times, the set is
sheared along the flow, and re-enclosing the sheared set in a box at every
step admits points that no trajectory reaches. The remedy that fits the
decomposition is to enclose the \emph{pass map}---entry state to exit
state, in the rescaled time of the profile---as a single object, a
zonotope or Taylor-model enclosure of a map rather than a tube of boxes,
and to compose pass maps with the outer flow.

\emph{Success.} A bound on the growth of the enclosure per pass, explicit
in the interval widths, and its verification on a multi-pass corridor
against sampled trajectories.
\end{openitem}

\subsection{Orthogonal scales, and what does not concentrate}
\label{sec:composition}
Two things have to be proved before the decomposition is a bound, and they
differ in kind.

The first concerns the passes. In the wave-equation theory two profiles are
orthogonal when their scales diverge or, at equal scale, when their centers
separate by a diverging number of widths~\cite[(1.18)]{bahouri1999high}.
Here the scale of a profile is the width of a pass and its center the time
at which the pass occurs, so passes at distinct times are orthogonal with
nothing to prove. The dissipation is a measure, so it splits
\emph{additively} across orthogonal profiles; that is the analogue of the
energy identity of that theory, and it costs nothing. The state does not
split additively, because an ordinary differential equation composes: the
state leaving one pass is the datum of the next arc. What has to be proved
is the analogue of the nonlinear statement of that theory, that the
contributions of separate profiles do not interact up to a small
error~\cite[(1.26)]{bahouri1999high}. Here it reads: the map from entry
data to exit data factors through the individual pass maps and the flows
between them, up to a remainder that is explicit and uniform over the
family. This is a matching estimate between inner and outer solutions. The
classical theory supplies its form, for one
trajectory~\cite{shi1971skip,kuo1995improved}, and its small parameter is
the one of \cref{sec:loss}: inside a pass gravity is negligible against the
aerodynamic force in proportion as $\gamma_e^2/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ is
large, which is why a pass below the threshold
cannot be treated as a jump at all. For the passes the argument worth
transporting from the wave-equation theory is the constructive one:
splitting a bounded sequence into an oscillatory and a singular part at
each scale forces those scales to be orthogonal, and iterating extracts a
pairwise orthogonal family, so that orthogonality is produced by the
construction and not assumed by it~\cite{bahouri1999high}.

\begin{openitem}{Composition of passes}
\label{obj:composition}
\emph{Question.} Does a trajectory with several passes factor through the
pass maps and the flows between them, with a remainder that is explicit and
uniform over the family?

\emph{To prove.} A non-interaction theorem for passes separated in time,
for every selection of the interval field, with a matching error at each
pass whose constant is in closed form. The statement has a range of
validity, $\gamma_e^2/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ above a threshold, and through
the relation between the turn of a pass and the energy it
sheds that threshold is the $\delta$ of
\cref{eq:kmax}. Passes below it are not composed but averaged.

\emph{Success.} The factorization with its remainder, for the translational
state under interval disturbances, verified against sampled multi-pass
trajectories.
\end{openitem}

The second concerns what does not concentrate. The attitude and the elastic
motions are not events. They are oscillations, at the short-period attitude
and first structural periods $T_{\mathrm{att}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{ela}}$,
measured against the slow time $T_{\mathrm{slow}} = \sqrt{R_e/g}$ by
$\eps_{\mathrm{att}} = T_{\mathrm{att}}/T_{\mathrm{slow}}$ and
$\eps_{\mathrm{ela}} = T_{\mathrm{ela}}/T_{\mathrm{slow}}$, and they persist
for as long as there is air. So does the oscillation about the glide,
whenever the loads are bounded. In a profile decomposition motions of this
kind are not profiles. They stay in the remainder, which the wave-equation
theory is careful to say is not small in energy, since it contains every
wave that concentrating profiles cannot
represent~\cite[p.~136]{bahouri1999high}. The tool for them is
averaging~\cite{khalil2002nonlinear,sanders2007averaging}: each oscillation
is carried by a slowly varying amplitude, and the slower dynamics see it
through its mean. Two things stand between that tool and a bound. An
averaging theorem gives the error to an order, and an order is not a
bound; the standard text says as much of its own
estimates~\cite[Sec.~10.1]{khalil2002nonlinear}. And with several
oscillations the theorem holds only away from resonance, while the
frequencies here are not constants. That of the attitude grows with the
square root of the dynamic pressure, so it rises from nothing at the start
of every pass, and all of them are known only to within the aerodynamic
intervals. Where two frequencies cross, averaging over both phases fails in
a zone about the resonance; the treatment there is itself an inner
expansion matched to an outer one, and a complete theory is not available
even for a single smooth field~\cite[Chs.~7--8]{sanders2007averaging}. The
results that pass through a resonance without resolving it hold, where
capture is possible, outside a set of initial conditions of small measure,
and an outer bound can discard nothing. This is the obligation for which
no theory is at hand, and it sits on the critical path.

\begin{openitem}{Averaging over the oscillations, through resonance}
\label{obj:averaging}
\emph{Question.} Can the oscillations that do not concentrate---about the
glide, of the attitude, of the structure---be averaged with an error that
is explicit, uniform over the selections of the interval field, and valid
for every trajectory, including those that pass through a resonance?

\emph{To prove.} An averaging theorem with constants. The amplitude of each
oscillation is carried as a slow coordinate of the limit system of
\cref{sec:bound} or enclosed in the interval field, and the theorem bounds
the distance between the slow coordinates of the full system and those of
the averaged inclusion, in closed form. The interval widths enter twice: as
a disturbance of each oscillation, and through the frequencies, which
depend on the uncertain coefficients, so that the ordering of the frequency
bands is itself a hypothesis to be verified over the corridor. Where two
bands cross, the statement is for the coupled pair, carried jointly through
the crossing, with the width of the resonance zone and the change of
amplitude across it enclosed.

\emph{Success.} The theorem for the oscillation about the glide and for the
attitude, with the remainder in closed form; the elastic modes either
included or enclosed as a widened interval, as in the fallback of
\cref{sec:plan}.
\end{openitem}

\subsection{The certified bound}\label{sec:bound}
Skeleton and bubbles together are not a surface with corrections but a
system of its own, with flows and jumps, and the target is stated against
it. In the
slow coordinates the \emph{limit system} $\mathcal{H}_\delta$ has two flows
and one jump. On an arc the state moves under gravity alone. On the glide
it moves along the balance $\Lambda = 1$, under the admissible bank
histories and the selections of the interval field, and it carries one
coordinate more: the amplitude of the oscillation about the balance. A pass
that dissipates more than the threshold~$\delta$ of \cref{eq:kmax} is a
jump. The state that enters it is sent to the set $J(x)$ of states that can
leave it, onto an arc or onto the glide, with the time and the range the
pass consumes included, and a trajectory makes at most $K_\delta$ of them.
The system has the form the limit dictates, and its data---the pass map,
the glide---are those of the given~$\eps$. The target statement is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target}
  \calR_\eps(T)
  \;\subseteq\;
  \Pi\bigl(\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}(T)\bigr)
  \;\oplus\;
  \B\bigl(\varrho(\eps,\delta)\bigr),
\end{equation}
uniformly over the admissible family generated by the uncertainty set, not
along a nominal trajectory. Here $\calR_\eps(T)$ is the set of slow states
reachable at time~$T$ from the initial set, under every admissible control
and every selection of the interval field, and $\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}(T)$
is the corresponding set for the limit system. $\Pi$ reads a state of the
limit system back in the coordinates of the full one: it is the identity on
an arc, and on the glide it returns the altitudes and flight-path angles
that an oscillation of the carried amplitude visits. The sum $\oplus$ is
the Minkowski sum, and $\B(\varrho)$ is the ball of radius~$\varrho$ in the
corridor metric, a weighted supremum norm in which altitude is measured in
scale heights and each remaining slow coordinate in units of its corridor
width. Passes compose in sequence, as jumps do, and their effects are not
added: an arc that meets the atmosphere has no continuation without the
pass. \Cref{fig:target} draws the statement on the ground.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/reachable-set.pdf}
  \caption{The target statement \cref{eq:target}, drawn on the ground. The
    trajectory is the example of \cref{fig:skip}, with altitude drawn twelve
    times too large and a post each minute. The three sets are an
    illustration and are not computed: what the vehicle can reach, what the
    limit system can reach, and the second thickened by the margin, which is
    the bound. The first may leave the second, but by no more than the
    margin.}
  \label{fig:target}
\end{figure}

\Cref{eq:target} is a statement about sets. As a statement about measures
it is the claim of \cref{obj:embedding}, that the occupation measures
converge to measures satisfying the Liouville equation of
$\mathcal{H}_\delta$, with the mass that crosses each pass carried by a
measure on the graph of~$J$. And the dual pair $(\cert,\certT)$ of
\cref{sec:embedding}, non-decreasing along the two flows and across the
jump, is the form in which an outer bound on
$\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}(T)$ is certified.

That the glide carries an amplitude is forced by the dynamics. Write $z$
for the altitude above the glide in scale heights and $G = g - V^2/r$ for
the acceleration the lift has to balance. Lift falls off exponentially with
altitude and $G$ does not, so to leading order in $\eps$, at frozen speed
and with the damping left out,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscillator-body}
  \ddot z \;=\; \frac{G}{H_s}\,\bigl(e^{-z} - 1\bigr) .
\end{equation}
The glide is its equilibrium. The phugoid, the oscillation of altitude
against flight-path angle about the glide, is its small oscillation, at the
frequency $\sqrt{G/H_s}$, which grows like $\eps^{-1/2}$. A skip is its
large one: a rebound from the exponential wall below and a ballistic arc
under $-G$ above, which is how the classical theory describes skip paths,
as oscillatory paths of large amplitude~\cite{shi1971skip}. An oscillation
that crosses the glide altitude at a flight-path angle $\gamma_0$ has an
amplitude fixed by $\gamma_0^2/\eps$, the parameter of the scaling in
\cref{sec:loss}. The two limits told apart there are the two ends of one
family, and the threshold~$\delta$ is a cut in the amplitude: an
oscillation above it is resolved pass by pass, and one below it is
averaged.

What the equation leaves out decides the analysis. With $L/D$ the
lift-to-drag ratio and $\sigma$ the bank angle, the damping rate of the
phugoid is $g/[V(L/D)\cos\sigma]$. It does not depend on $\eps$, while the
frequency grows like $\eps^{-1/2}$. In the limit the motion normal to the
glide is an undamped oscillation on its own timescale: the glide balance is
not a normally hyperbolic slow manifold in the sense the geometric theory
requires~\cite{fenichel1979geometric}, and no boundary-layer estimate of
Tikhonov type brings a trajectory onto it. Nor is the amplitude small. A
change of bank moves the glide altitude by a fixed fraction of a scale
height however thin the layer, and a bank history in step with the
oscillation pumps it, as one opposed to it damps it.
Guidance laws use the second~\cite{lu2014entry}, and a reachable set has to
allow for the first. The amplitude is therefore a coordinate on which the
control acts, and its range belongs to $\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}$, not to
the residual.

The residual $\varrho(\eps,\delta)$ collects what the limit system does not
carry, and it has three parts: the lag of a trajectory behind the
descending glide, the matching error at each of at most $K_\delta$ passes,
and the averaging error of the oscillations. It and the count $K_\delta$
are to be derived as explicit, closed-form expressions in the corridor
bounds and the aerodynamic interval widths, independent of any numerical
optimization. One piece is immediate: the count is \cref{eq:kmax}. The
rest is to be derived, and what to expect of the first part can be said.
The lag, in scale heights, should be of order $\sqrt{\eps}$, the order of
the damping ratio of the phugoid, with a constant in the speed, the
lift-to-drag ratio and the bank that degenerates as $V$ approaches circular
speed, which is where the glide gives way to arcs and passes. It would be
the ringing amplitude of a trajectory started level on the glide, and no
more than that: it says nothing of a trajectory that leaves a pass, which
rings with whatever amplitude the pass left it, for a time comparable to
the entry. That amplitude is what the limit system carries.

In the spherical-LVLH coordinates the altitude and speed bounds of the
corridor are box constraints on $(r,V)$. The heating, dynamic-pressure and
load limits are not: each is a curve $h \ge h_{\min}(V)$. Each is, however,
monotone in altitude and in speed at fixed attitude, so contracting a box
against it returns the exact interval hull of the feasible part, and the
interval widths that enter $\varrho$ and $K_\delta$ inherit that
tightness. Requiring the constants in closed
form is not what makes the bound a proof; a verified solver output is one
too (\cref{sec:provenance}). It is what makes the dependence on the
corridor and on the interval widths explicit, so that the bound is
evaluated again, and not solved for again, when either changes.

\begin{openitem}{Closed-form constants}
\label{obj:constants}
\emph{Question.} What are $K_\delta$ and $\varrho(\eps,\delta)$ as
functions of the corridor bounds, the initial set and the aerodynamic
interval widths?

\emph{To derive.} The count from the energy budget, with the threshold
$\delta$ chosen against the residual it leaves behind: lowered, it resolves
more passes one by one and pays a matching error for each; raised, it
leaves larger oscillations to be averaged. The residual in its three parts:
the lag behind the glide; the
matching error of \cref{obj:composition}; and the averaging error of
\cref{obj:averaging}. Each constant is to hold for every selection of the
interval field, and its range of validity---bounded away from circular
speed---is part of the statement.

\emph{Success.} Formulas whose evaluation over the corridor in validated
interval arithmetic bounds the distance from sampled trajectories of the
full model to the reachable set of the limit system, with no fitted number.
\end{openitem}

\section{The application: hypersonic entry}\label{sec:application}
%==========================================================================
Entry is not an arbitrary illustration of the machinery above. It supplies
the singular perturbation, the limit system and the concentrating events in
one system, and the existing methods, for the reasons that follow, stop
short of it.

\subsection{Why existing methods do not reach it}
The direct method solves a Hamilton--Jacobi--Isaacs equation on a grid,
which is exponential in state dimension and capped in practice near four or
five states~\parencite{bansal2017hamilton,mitchell2005time}.
A rigid-body entry vehicle carries thirteen states,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:state-rigid}
  x = (r,\lambda,\phi,\, V,\gamma,\psi,\,
       q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3,\, p,q_b,r_b)
  \;\in\;\R^{13},
\end{equation}
and more once the heat load, the mass, the thermal response and recession
of the heat shield and the aerothermoelastic modes are
coupled~\parencite{mcnamara2011aeroelastic}. The two counts use
``degree of freedom'' in its two customary senses, and it is worth
separating them: the vehicle is six-degree-of-freedom in the
configuration sense (three translational, three rotational), while
\cref{eq:state-rigid} has thirteen coordinates spanning a
twelve-dimensional state manifold once the unit-quaternion constraint
$\norm{\vvec{q}} = 1$ is imposed.

So the grid method stops before the real problem starts.

Set-propagation methods go further. Taylor-model and zonotope flowpipes
return rigorous outer bounds at ten to twenty states, and they are the
baseline this program has to be measured
against~\cite{chen2013flowstar,althoff2021set,makino2003taylor}. What they
return is an enclosure of one instance: a numerical set for one vehicle,
one initial box and one horizon, whose tightness tends to degrade with the
width of the initial set and with the length of weakly damped motion it has
to follow---and the phugoid is weakly damped for the whole of the glide.
They are the right tool inside a pass, where the horizon is short, and that
is how \cref{obj:passmaps} uses them. They do not produce a bound whose
dependence on the corridor and on the interval widths is explicit, which is
what a statement uniform over a family requires.

In practice the question is answered by sampling. Entry guidance has
converged on numerical predictor--corrector algorithms whose performance is
established by simulation over dispersed cases~\cite{lu2014entry}: a
sample of the family, and not an enclosure of it.

What makes the measure-theoretic route viable is not that it evades the
dimension but that the bound it produces does not scale with it: the limit
system is low-dimensional by construction, each pass enters through one
map, and the residual is a single ball.

\subsection{The state space and the corridor}
The translational state is carried in geocentric spherical position
$(r,\lambda,\phi)$ and wind-frame velocity $(V,\gamma,\psi)$, so that the
altitude and speed bounds of the entry corridor are box constraints on
states and the heating, dynamic-pressure and load limits are monotone in
the two coordinates they bind---the natural setting for interval
contraction. Attitude is a unit quaternion, free of Euler-angle
singularities. The chart has a price, which is stated rather than hidden:
the longitude and heading rates are singular at the geographic poles, so
the corridor is taken with $\abs{\phi}\le\phi_{\max}<\pi/2$, and a
trajectory class that overflies a pole needs a second chart or a Cartesian
formulation.

Because interval arithmetic evaluates transcendentals natively, the
algebraic adjunctions that recast the system in polynomial
form~\cite{savageau1987recasting} are not needed on the main line; they
survive only inside the moment-sum-of-squares cross-check. The state space
therefore carries only the physical dimensions---spherical position,
wind-frame velocity, attitude quaternion, body rates, thermal, ablation,
and modal states---with no artificial embeddings, and the two auxiliary
states of the polynomial formulation, the density root
$y = \sqrt{\rho/\rho_0}$ and the wall-temperature surrogate, are
gone. This is a design decision rather than an open
problem, and its trade is stated once: transcendental dynamics in
coordinates native to the corridor and the glide, against polynomial
dynamics in which the corridor is a nonlinear function of the
state.

\begin{openitem}{Independent verification}
\label{obj:verification}
\emph{Question.} Is the certificate right, by evidence that does not share
its derivation?

\emph{To carry out.} Three checks, in the order of \cref{sec:provenance}.
Symbolic verification of every derivation behind the constants. A
moment-sum-of-squares outer approximation on a reduced
polynomial model, as a cross-check that shares neither the decomposition
nor the interval arithmetic. And a comparison on a common benchmark---a
capsule skip entry reconstructed from published data---against Taylor-model
flowpipes and against sampled trajectories of the full model, reported for
each coordinate group rather than as a single number.

\emph{Success.} Containment of every sample by the certified set, agreement
with the cross-check where both apply, and a stated tightness against the
baseline, coordinate by coordinate.
\end{openitem}

\section{Plan of work}\label{sec:plan}
%==========================================================================
Seven concrete obligations carry the program; they are stated where each
arises above and gathered here, in order, as the plan of work.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Interval embedding, uniform through the singular limit}: what
    the occupation measures of the interval inclusion converge to, and the
    Liouville constraint, with its jump term, that the limit satisfies.
  \item \emph{Validated evaluation of the certificate}: interval evaluation,
    contraction and integration that discharge the certificate with every
    rounding error accounted for, and that enclose the reachable set of the
    limit system.
  \item \emph{Enclosures of the pass maps}: tight enclosures carried through
    a sequence of atmospheric passes without premature blow-up.
  \item \emph{Composition of passes}: the factorization of a trajectory
    through the pass maps and the flows between them, with an explicit
    matching remainder.
  \item \emph{Averaging over the oscillations, through resonance}: an
    averaging theorem with constants, uniform over the selections and valid
    where frequencies cross---\textbf{the critical path}, on which the
    reduction to a limit system and the closed-form bound rest.
  \item \emph{Closed-form constants}: the count $K_\delta$ and the residual
    $\varrho(\eps,\delta)$ from the physical corridor and the interval
    widths, free of any numerical solver.
  \item \emph{Independent verification}: symbolic checks of the
    derivations, a moment-sum-of-squares cross-check, and a benchmark
    against set-propagation tools and sampled trajectories.
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{obj:embedding} states the limit;
\cref{obj:passmaps,obj:composition,obj:averaging} establish the
decomposition; \cref{obj:constants} reduces it to closed form;
\cref{obj:pipeline,obj:verification} compute with it and check it.
\Cref{obj:averaging} gates the rest.

\subsection{Sequencing}
The dependency structure suggests three phases rather than seven parallel
tracks. The first establishes the embedding---\cref{obj:embedding},
together with the certified envelope of the atmospheric density that it
needs in order to be stated on a concrete inclusion.
The second attacks the critical path: \cref{obj:averaging}, which is where
the program either works or does not, and which should therefore be reached
early enough that a negative result is still recoverable. Its first case is
small and self-contained, the planar oscillator of
\cref{eq:oscillator-body} with its damping, its drift and a bank history
acting on it, and it can be started at once. \Cref{obj:passmaps,obj:composition}
run alongside, since the matching argument and the enclosure geometry
constrain each other. The third phase reduces the decomposition to closed
form---\cref{obj:constants}---completes the validated evaluation of
\cref{obj:pipeline}, and runs the symbolic and moment-sum-of-squares checks
of \cref{obj:verification} against the result. The validated evaluation is
the one part of the program that a weaker theorem does not shorten.

Two retreat positions are worth identifying now. If \cref{obj:averaging}
resists a proof where frequencies cross, the fallback is a corridor on
which the bands are ordered---a floor on the dynamic pressure, a ceiling on
the speed below circular---with the crossings at the edges of each pass
left to the pass enclosures of \cref{obj:passmaps}, which integrate the
coupled field and need no separation, and with the elastic modes treated as
a widened interval. That weakens the residual without invalidating the
decomposition. And if the amplitude of the oscillation about the glide
cannot be bounded over every bank history, the statement is made for bank
histories that vary slowly against the oscillation, for which the amplitude
decays. That restricts the family and keeps the bound explicit.
