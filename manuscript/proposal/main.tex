\begin{abstract}
Computing the reachable set of a six-degree-of-freedom hypersonic entry
vehicle is intractable for grid-based dynamic programming, and a bound
produced by a numerical solver inherits the solver's accuracy. We propose
an analytical framework for certified outer bounds on such reachable sets.
The dynamics are embedded in a space of occupation measures, where they
become a linear constraint, and the transcendental aerodynamics are
enclosed in intervals, so that the vector field is a differential inclusion
evaluated in validated interval arithmetic. The new content is the
thin-atmosphere limit. In this limit the occupation measures converge, but
their limit forgets every atmospheric pass: a pass lasts a vanishing time,
and yet it is where the trajectories of the family are told apart. We read
the loss on the energy dissipation along the rescaled time axis, where the
concentration-compactness principle of Lions applies, and extract a profile
decomposition: a low-dimensional skeleton, formed by the arcs above the
atmosphere and the equilibrium glide; one boundary-layer bubble per
atmospheric pass; and a residual. The target statement is that the
reachable set is contained in that of the limit system formed by the
skeleton and the bubbles, thickened by a ball whose radius is explicit. The
constants that size the bound are to be derived in closed form from the
physical corridor and the aerodynamic interval widths. The bound then holds
uniformly over the entire admissible family, in the minimal physical state
space, and it is re-evaluated rather than re-solved when the vehicle or the
corridor changes. The central theorem is open, and this document claims no
result: it states the problem, the target statement, and the plan of work.
\end{abstract}

\section{The analytical problem}
%==========================================================================
The object to be bounded is the reachable set of an atmospheric entry
vehicle, that is, every state it can be driven to under admissible control.
This is what a safety analysis needs enclosed. We defer the flight-mechanics
specifics to \cref{sec:application}, which explains why entry is the right
instance and describes its state space. What matters first is that the
problem is an analytical one before it is an aerospace one, and that the
existing methods fall short of it for two independent reasons: the
dimension of the state, discussed with the application in
\cref{sec:application}, and the provenance of the guarantee, discussed in
\cref{sec:provenance}. There are therefore two problems to solve, not one.

The approach has four steps. The first is the \emph{embedding}: the coupled
flight dynamics are written with the aerodynamics enclosed in intervals, so
that they form a differential inclusion. The second is the
\emph{relaxation}: the inclusion is lifted to a linear problem on occupation
measures (\cref{sec:embedding}). The third is the \emph{decomposition}: in
the thin-atmosphere limit the family of measures loses exactly the events
that matter, and we recover them through a profile decomposition into a
skeleton, bubbles and a residual (\cref{sec:profile}). The fourth is the
\emph{certification}: the constants of the resulting bound are derived in
closed form and evaluated in validated interval arithmetic
(\cref{sec:provenance,sec:bound}). Seven objectives carry the program. They
are stated where each arises and gathered in \cref{sec:plan}.

\subsection{Reachable sets as occupation measures}\label{sec:embedding}
We embed the flight dynamics in a space of occupation measures. Consider
the control system $\dot x = f(x,c,t)$, with the state $x$ in the flight
corridor $\calX$ and the control $c$ in the admissible set $\calU$. Along a
trajectory, the time spent in each set defines an occupation measure $\mu$
on $[0,T]\times\calX\times\calU$. In terms of $\mu$ the nonlinear flight
mechanics become \emph{linear}: the Liouville equation is a linear
constraint relating $\mu$ to an initial measure and to a terminal measure
$\mu_T$, and the reachable set at time~$T$ is the union of the supports
$\operatorname{supp}\mu_T$ over all measures that satisfy it. An outer bound
is obtained from the dual problem. A pair of functions $(\cert,\certT)$, the
first non-decreasing along every admissible trajectory and at least one on
the initial set, the second majorizing the first at time~$T$, encloses the
reachable set in the superlevel set $\{\certT\ge 1\}$. So far this is the
occupation-measure program of Lasserre, Henrion and
collaborators~\cite{lasserre2008nonlinear,vinter1993convex}. It was
developed for regions of attraction and invariant
sets~\cite{henrion2014convex,korda2014convex} and, for hybrid systems, for
reachable sets and optimal control, where a measure on each guard carries
the mass from one mode to the next~\cite{shia2014convex,zhao2020optimal}.

The embedding buys linearity at the price of a relaxation. A measure that
satisfies the Liouville constraint is generated by a family of trajectories
of the \emph{convexified} inclusion
$\dot x\in\operatorname{conv} f(x,\calU,t)$, and not necessarily by a single
trajectory of the original system~\cite[Lemma~3]{henrion2014convex}. Since
the relaxed reachable set contains the true one, an outer bound on the
first is an outer bound on the second, which is the direction we need.

The same relaxation admits the interval envelope. The true aerodynamics are
transcendental and known only to within an envelope, so we enclose them in
intervals. A vector field selected from an interval is a set-valued map, and
the resulting differential inclusion, not the nominal field, is the object
that the measure must
describe~\cite{filippov1988differential,aubin1984differential}. If the
selection is written as an additional box-valued input, the inclusion is a
control system with a bounded disturbance, and at fixed $\eps$ its Liouville
constraint is the standard one. What is not standard is to carry the outer
approximation through the singular limit of \cref{sec:profile}, uniformly
over the selections.

The nearest prior art is the occupation-measure theory of singularly
perturbed inclusions~\cite{artstein1999invariant}. Separating time scales is
a standard device in flight mechanics~\cite{naidu2001singular}, and the
classical reduction, which sets $\eps=0$, requires the fast motion to
settle. The theory of~\cite{artstein1999invariant} does not. It defines an
invariant measure for the fast motion without assuming uniqueness of
solutions, and it lets the slow dynamics be driven by these invariant
measures. Its hypotheses show where the entry problem departs from it. The
linear growth condition on the field only excludes finite-time blow-up, and
it is automatic on a bounded corridor. The singular-perturbation theorem,
however, assumes a uniform bound on the fast state along the whole
$\eps$-family~\cite{artstein1999invariant,artstein2000value}. The natural
fast state of entry is the altitude measured in scale heights. On a corridor
of fixed altitude extent this variable ranges over an interval of length
proportional to $1/\eps$, the aerodynamic terms grow exponentially in it,
and a skip that rises through a fixed altitude carries it out of every
bounded set. The hypothesis therefore holds for motion that stays within a
bounded number of scale heights of the glide, and it fails exactly where an
atmospheric pass concentrates (\cref{sec:loss}).

\begin{openitem}{Liouville equation for the interval inclusion}
\label{obj:embedding}
At fixed $\eps$ the interval inclusion $\dot x\in\calF_\eps(x,c,t)$ is a
control system with a box-valued disturbance, and its Liouville constraint
is standard. So are the bounds that hold uniformly in $\eps$ on mass, on
support and, through the energy balance, on dissipation. Two things are not
standard. First, we must construct a certified upper and lower envelope of
the atmospheric density, which fixes $\calF_\eps$ concretely. Second, we
must identify the limit of the constraint itself. A weak-$*$ limit of the
measures satisfies the Liouville equation of the limit flows only up to a
defect, which is carried where the dissipation concentrates. We must prove
that this defect is a jump term, on a measure supported on the graph of the
pass map, so that the limit satisfies the Liouville equation of the limit
system of \cref{sec:bound} in the form with guard
measures~\cite{shia2014convex,zhao2020optimal}, uniformly over the
selections. Where nothing concentrates, the limit is of the kind that the
existing theory describes, with the oscillation about the glide represented
by an invariant measure~\cite{artstein1999invariant}. The objective is met
when the limit constraint is stated for the full inclusion, with each
hypothesis either verified on the entry corridor or named as an assumption,
together with its dual: a pair $(\cert,\certT)$ that is non-decreasing along
the flows and across the jumps of the limit system.
\end{openitem}

\subsection{The provenance of the guarantee}\label{sec:provenance}
A polynomial barrier whose coefficients come from an interior-point solver
has analytical \emph{form}, but it inherits the solver's
accuracy~\cite{prajna2007framework}. The documented failure modes of
numerical semidefinite solvers include the loss of strict feasibility, on
which the repair schemes depend~\cite{roux2018validating}. A solver's output
can be made into a certificate: if it is rounded to rational coefficients
and re-verified exactly, it is a proof~\cite{peyrl2008computing}. The
obstacle is scale rather than principle. The rounding needs a strictly
feasible Gram matrix, which a tight bound does not have, and exact rational
certificates carry a bit-complexity whose exponent grows with the number of
variables~\cite{kaltofen2012exact,magron2021exact}. For the thirteen-state
field of \cref{sec:application} we do not pursue this route as the main
line.

Instead, we fix an order of evidence. The certificate itself is a
closed-form bound: the constants of \cref{sec:profile}, derived from the
decomposition and evaluated over the corridor in validated interval
arithmetic. Behind it stand symbolic checks of the derivations on which
those constants rest. A moment-sum-of-squares computation on a reduced model
is the third tier. It serves as an independent cross-check, to which the
exact-certification machinery above belongs, and it is never the source of
the guarantee.

Interval arithmetic is the setting in which a guarantee has meant, for sixty
years, an enclosure rather than an approximation. It is rigorous in floating
point, because every operation is rounded outward and the computed interval
contains the exact result. Exact rational arithmetic would add nothing to
soundness, and it cannot evaluate an exponential or a
sine~\cite[Ch.~3]{moore2009introduction}. Two distinct losses have to be
managed. The first is the \emph{dependency problem}. Interval arithmetic is
inclusion isotonic, which is the property that makes an enclosure valid, but
repeated occurrences of a variable are treated as independent, and
distributivity weakens to
subdistributivity~\cite[Chs.~4--5]{moore2009introduction}. The second is the
\emph{wrapping effect}. The image of a box under a flow is not a box, and
re-enclosing it at every step admits points that no trajectory
reaches~\cite[Ch.~10]{moore2009introduction}. Refining the time step does
not remove it. What controls wrapping is the representation of the set, for
example Lohner's QR factorization, zonotopes, or Taylor
models~\cite{nedialkov1999validated,makino2003taylor}. Applied interval
analysis supplies the remaining machinery: certified set inversion,
contractors, and validated integration~\cite{jaulin2001applied}.

\begin{openitem}{Validated evaluation of the certificate}
\label{obj:pipeline}
The guarantee is to be verified in validated arithmetic rather than produced
by a numerical solver. We must show that the closed-form constants, the pass
enclosures that they do not cover, and the reachable set of the limit system
of \cref{sec:bound} can be evaluated over the whole corridor with every
rounding error accounted for. This is a computer-assisted step of the proof:
the transcendental field and its Jacobian are enclosed with outward
rounding, the corridor constraints are contracted, and the passes are
integrated with validated error bounds. The limit system is itself a
reachability problem, in a few slow coordinates and over every bank history.
It is to be enclosed by comparison bounds in closed form where they exist,
and by validated set propagation where they do not. The objective is met
when every inequality that the certificate asserts is discharged by a
computation that returns either a proof or an explicit failure to decide at
the working precision.
\end{openitem}

\section{The singular limit and its profile decomposition}
\label{sec:profile}
%==========================================================================
This section carries the new content. The entry problem supplies a small
parameter, the thin-atmosphere ratio $\eps_{\mathrm{atm}} = H_s/R_e$, where
$H_s$ is the atmospheric scale height and $R_e$ the planetary radius. We
write $\eps$ for it where the other small parameters are not in play. The
question is what the family of occupation measures does as $\eps\to 0$.

\subsection{Loss of compactness along the time axis and the defect measure}
\label{sec:loss}
On the corridor nothing is lost in the usual sense. The set
$[0,T]\times\calX\times\calU$ is compact, so the occupation measures, which
are nonnegative Radon measures in $\Mplus([0,T]\times\calX\times\calU)$ with
the weak-$*$ topology, form a tight family and converge along subsequences.
The limit is nevertheless the wrong object, because it forgets the
atmospheric passes (\cref{fig:skip}).

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/skip-entry.pdf}
  \caption{A skip entry at the physical scale height. The example is the
    lunar return of Artemis~I, simulated from the published entry
    state~\cite{rea2024orion}. \textbf{Top:} altitude. \textbf{Bottom:} the
    drag power per unit mass~$P$, which is the rate at which mechanical
    energy is dissipated. The shaded intervals are the two atmospheric
    passes, on which $P$ exceeds one tenth of its peak; between them the
    vehicle lofts and dissipates little. An occupation measure weighs every
    interval by its length, whereas what distinguishes one trajectory from
    another is decided in the shaded intervals, which have no length in the
    limit.}
  \label{fig:skip}
\end{figure}

How much the limit forgets depends on which limit is taken, and we address
this first. A pass entered at a flight-path angle $\gamma_e$ turns the
velocity through an angle of about $2\gamma_e$. Since lift cannot be had
without drag, it also sheds a share of the kinetic energy of order
$\gamma_e/(L/D)$, where $L/D$ is the lift-to-drag ratio. The pass crosses a
scale height in a time $H_s/(V\gamma_e)$, which is of order
$\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}/\gamma_e$ on the slow time scale, and it levels out
where the lift is $V^2\gamma_e^2/(2H_s)$, which is of order
$\gamma_e^2/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ relative to gravity. The entry angle
therefore enters through the ratio $\gamma_e/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$. As
in the classical Chapman--Loh variables, it is
$\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$ and not $\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ that sets the
scale~\cite{chapman1959approximate,loh1968reentry}. Two limits must be
distinguished.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item If $\gamma_e/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$ is held fixed, a pass lasts
    a time of order $\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$, sheds an energy of the
    same order, and loads the vehicle by a fixed multiple of the glide load.
    This is the limit of a corridor whose dynamic-pressure and load limits
    are held fixed. In it nothing concentrates: the passes are many and
    slight, and they are the troughs of an oscillation about the glide.
  \item If $\gamma_e$ is held fixed, a pass lasts a time of order
    $\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ and sheds a fixed share of the energy, under loads
    that grow like $1/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$. It carries occupation mass of
    that order, and none in the limit, while its share of the energy
    dissipated and of the turn of the velocity vector does not vanish.
\end{itemize}
The physical case lies between the two. Since
$\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}\approx 0.034$ is about two degrees and entry
angles are a few degrees, a pass need be neither short compared with the arc
that follows it nor distinct in scale from the oscillation about the glide.
The statement that we aim at is therefore one at fixed $\eps$, with a
threshold that decides which passes are resolved one by one, and the two
limits are the extremes that it has to cover.

A reachable set is a support and not an integral, so what has no mass still
counts. This alone does not distinguish the problem from the limit theory of
singularly perturbed control, whose value functions already carry a terminal
cost on the slow state~\cite{artstein2000value}. The difference lies in the
hypothesis under which that theory works: the fast state, and the
trajectories that approximate an optimum, must stay in a bounded set, and a
pass that concentrates is exactly what this excludes. We therefore read the
loss where it occurs, along the fast-time axis
$\tau = t/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}$. On this axis the horizon
$[0,T/\sqrt{\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}}]$ exhausts the half-line and translations
act non-compactly, so compactness is lost in the same way as for a problem
posed on an unbounded domain.

Between passes the vehicle is outside the sensible atmosphere, on an arc
governed by gravity alone. Once captured, it stays near the
equilibrium-glide balance $\Lambda = 1$. In the three-degree-of-freedom
(point-mass) approximation this is the scalar condition
$L\cos\sigma/[m(g - V^2/r)] = 1$, where $\sigma$ is the bank angle. In the
full six-degree-of-freedom formulation the lift component $L\cos\sigma$ is
not an independent input but is determined by the attitude quaternion
through the body-to-LVLH rotation. The balance is then a single scalar
constraint on the thirteen-dimensional translational-rotational state, and
the slow set is its intersection with the trim and quasi-static-deflection
conditions of the two faster motions. We take the balance with planetary
rotation and oblate gravity retained. The two do not enter on equal terms:
flying east at entry speed, the Coriolis term is tens of percent of the net
acceleration $g - V^2/r$ that the lift has to balance, while the $J_2$ terms
are at most a percent of it. The scalar condition $\Lambda = 1$ is the
reduction under spherical gravity, no rotation, and small flight-path angle.

The loss is read on a \emph{defect measure}, which must be chosen so that
the passes carry mass. The part of the occupation-measure limit that lies
off the glide cannot serve, since the passes have none. The energy
dissipation can. Along every trajectory of the inclusion, the specific
mechanical energy in the rotating frame,
$\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 V^2 + \Phi_g - \tfrac12\omega_E^2 r^2\cos^2\phi$,
changes at the rate $\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -P$, where $P = DV/m \ge 0$ is the
drag power, because neither the lift nor the Coriolis force does work. Here
$\Phi_g$ is the gravitational potential, $\omega_E$ the rotation rate of the
planet, and $\phi$ the latitude. Consequently, the total dissipation
satisfies $\int_0^T P\dd t \le \mathcal{E}_{\max} - \mathcal{E}_{\min}$,
uniformly over controls, over selections of the interval field, and over
$\eps$. We define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:defect}
  \nu_\eps(\tau)
  \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{\eps}\;P\bigl(\sqrt{\eps}\,\tau\bigr)}
             {\int_0^T P\dd t} ,
\end{equation}
the dissipation density on the fast-time axis, normalized to unit mass and
extended by zero. We propose $\nu_\eps$ as the defect measure. Where it
concentrates is where the limit of the occupation measures can fail to
satisfy the Liouville equation of the limit flow, and the mass that it puts
there should bound the size of the failure, because the aerodynamic impulse
over any interval is bounded by the energy dissipated in it.

Along any sequence $\eps_n\to 0$, with a trajectory chosen for each, the
$\nu_\eps$ are nonnegative densities of fixed mass on the line. This is
exactly the hypothesis of the concentration-compactness lemma of
Lions~\cite[Lemma~I.1]{lions1984concentration1}, which classifies such
sequences into three alternatives. Here each of them has a physical meaning:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \emph{vanishing}: no window of fixed fast-time width holds a fixed
    share of the dissipation. This is the glide and the oscillation about
    it, on which the drag is bounded and such a window holds a share of
    order $\sqrt{\eps}$;
  \item \emph{compactness}, up to translation: one pass carries the
    dissipation;
  \item \emph{dichotomy}: the dissipation splits between pieces whose
    fast-time separation diverges, that is, two passes a positive time
    apart, or a pass followed by a glide.
\end{itemize}
Which alternative occurs is decided by the scaling above. With the loads
held fixed, the drag power is bounded and every sequence vanishes.
Concentration requires an entry angle that stays large compared with
$\sqrt{\eps}$. In that case translations along the fast-time axis separate
the passes, and the dilation half of the principle, the limit-case
modification~\cite{lions1985limit1}, resolves each pass at its own width,
since a pass shorter than $\sqrt{\eps}$ is a Dirac mass at that scale. The
trichotomy is Lions' lemma applied as stated. That the vanishing part is the
glide and the concentrated pieces are the passes remains to be proved,
starting from the inner and outer expansions of the classical
theory~\cite{shi1971skip}.

Measures that keep track of concentration are not new to control theory. An
unbounded control that enters the dynamics affinely is relaxed to a measure
in time whose atoms are jumps of the state, and oscillation and
concentration of a control sequence have been treated together through the
measures of DiPerna and
Majda~\cite[and the references there]{claeys2017semidefinite}. In that
setting the concentration is put in by the control. Here the control is
bounded and the limit produces the concentration, through a force that
depends on the state, so the effect of a jump is the solution of an inner
problem. Bounding the impulse alone would give a limit that is sound but far
too large.

\subsection{Skeleton, bubbles, residual}
Rather than restore compactness, we extract the profile decomposition that
iterating the dichotomy provides:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a low-dimensional \emph{skeleton}, which is what survives the limit:
    the exo-atmospheric arcs between passes, which are conics up to the
    oblateness correction, followed by the equilibrium glide, a graph over
    $(r,V)$ in the corridor subspace once the balance is solved for the
    vertical lift fraction. For the glide, a rigorous characterization with
    quantitative accuracy estimates already
    exists~\cite{lu2006asymptotic}. It treats the glide as a \emph{regular}
    perturbation: the exact solution is not attracted to the zeroth-order
    one, a point that returns in \cref{sec:bound};

  \item one \emph{boundary-layer bubble} per atmospheric pass, the analogue
    of a concentrating profile: the pass rescaled to its own width. Its net
    effect on the slow coordinates is a jump. The exit states that the bank
    history and the selections can produce from one entry state form a
    compact set, and the map that assigns this set to the entry state, the
    \emph{pass map}~$J$, is the object to be enclosed. Orthogonality is read
    off the scale-and-core separation condition that governs concentrating
    profiles in general~\cite{gerard1998description}. The decomposition of
    skip entry into an aerodynamic inner region and a gravitational outer
    one is classical~\cite{shi1971skip,chapman1959approximate}, and it has
    been carried to second order and to three
    dimensions~\cite{kuo1995improved}. These are expansions of a single
    trajectory, with the error assessed by example. What is not classical
    is a statement that is uniform over a family of trajectories, with an
    explicit remainder at fixed $\eps$;

  \item a \emph{residual}, which is what the finitely many profiles do not
    capture. In a profile decomposition the remainder is not compact and is
    not small in the conserved norm; it is small in a weaker norm, and only
    as the number of extracted profiles
    grows~\cite{bahouri1999high,solimini1995note}. Here the weaker norm is
    the largest share of dissipation held by any fast-time window: on a
    glide the residual carries most of the dissipation, but almost none of
    it in any one window. The quantitative bound that we need is a different
    statement, a distance in state space, which we take up in
    \cref{sec:bound}.
\end{itemize}

We keep two kinds of statement apart, because they come from different
arguments. A decomposition obtained by compactness is a statement about
subsequences. What it gives uniformly, it gives by contradiction: it says
which pieces can occur and that nothing else can, and it produces no
constant. The wave-equation theory is explicit about this, since the uniform
bound that follows from its decomposition comes with a constant that its
authors say they cannot describe~\cite[Corollary~2]{bahouri1999high}. We
therefore ask compactness only for the structure. The constants are to come
from direct estimates: the energy identity, the bound that it gives on the
aerodynamic impulse, a matching estimate at each pass, and averaging over
the oscillations that do not concentrate. Each of these is an inequality
with a constant that can be written down, and writing them down is
\cref{obj:constants}.

The number of bubbles is bounded \emph{a~priori}, and the bound comes from
the same monotone quantity. A pass that dissipates at least $\delta$ of
specific energy can occur at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kmax}
  K_\delta \;\le\; \frac{\mathcal{E}_{\max}-\mathcal{E}_{\min}}{\delta}
\end{equation}
times, for every control and every selection of the interval field. Passes
below the threshold are left to the oscillation about the glide and to the
residual. This is the device of the profile-decomposition literature: only
finitely many profiles exceed any given size. The integrated heat-load
budget gives the count its physical interpretation, since a trajectory can
only afford so many skips~\cite{shi1971skip,eggers1958comparative}.
However, that budget depends on a stagnation-point heating correlation,
whereas the energy budget does not.

Enclosing a pass is where the wrapping effect has to be met. For linear
systems it can be avoided altogether: support-function methods propagate
each direction independently, so successive over-approximations do not
compound, and what remains is a discretization error of first order in the
time step~\cite{leguernic2010reachability}. Nothing comparable is available
for a nonlinear pass.

\begin{openitem}{Wrapping-effect suppression in boundary-layer bubbles}
\label{obj:passmaps}
Each boundary-layer bubble represents one atmospheric pass, and its net
effect on the slow coordinates must be enclosed tightly enough that a
sequence of $K_\delta$ passes does not exhaust the corridor. Wrapping in a
pass has a specific source. Trajectories of a set enter the layer at
different times, the set is sheared along the flow, and re-enclosing the
sheared set in a box at every step admits points that no trajectory reaches.
We must therefore enclose the pass map, from entry state to exit state in
the rescaled time of the profile, as a single object, using a zonotope or
Taylor-model enclosure of a map rather than a tube of boxes, and compose
pass maps with the outer flow. The objective is met by a bound on the growth
of the enclosure per pass that is explicit in the interval widths, verified
on a multi-pass corridor against sampled trajectories.
\end{openitem}

\subsection{Separation of scales, and what does not concentrate}
\label{sec:composition}
Two things must be proved before the decomposition is a bound, and they
differ in kind.

The first concerns the passes. In the wave-equation theory, two profiles are
orthogonal when their scales diverge or, at equal scale, when their centers
separate by a diverging number of widths~\cite[(1.18)]{bahouri1999high}.
Here the scale of a profile is the width of a pass and its center is the
time at which the pass occurs, so passes at distinct times are orthogonal
without further argument. Since the dissipation is a measure, it splits
\emph{additively} across orthogonal profiles, which is the analogue of the
energy identity of that theory. The state does not split additively, because
an ordinary differential equation composes: the state leaving one pass is
the initial datum of the next arc. What must be proved is the analogue of
the nonlinear statement of that theory, that the contributions of separate
profiles do not interact up to a small
error~\cite[(1.26)]{bahouri1999high}. Here it reads as follows: the map from
entry data to exit data factors through the individual pass maps and the
flows between them, up to a remainder that is explicit and uniform over the
family. This is a matching estimate between inner and outer solutions. The
classical theory supplies its form for one
trajectory~\cite{shi1971skip,kuo1995improved}, and its small parameter is
that of \cref{sec:loss}: inside a pass, gravity is negligible compared with
the aerodynamic force in proportion as $\gamma_e^2/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ is
large, which is why a pass below the threshold cannot be treated as a jump.
For the passes, the argument worth transporting from the wave-equation
theory is the constructive one. Splitting a bounded sequence into an
oscillatory and a singular part at each scale forces those scales to be
orthogonal, and iterating extracts a pairwise orthogonal family, so that
orthogonality is produced by the construction and not
assumed~\cite{bahouri1999high}.

\begin{openitem}{Composition of passes}
\label{obj:composition}
We must prove a non-interaction theorem for passes separated in time: a
trajectory with several passes factors through the pass maps and the flows
between them, for every selection of the interval field, with a matching
error at each pass whose constant is in closed form. The statement has a
range of validity, namely $\gamma_e^2/\eps_{\mathrm{atm}}$ above a
threshold, and through the relation between the turn of a pass and the
energy that it sheds, that threshold is the $\delta$ of \cref{eq:kmax}.
Passes below it are not composed but averaged. The objective is met by the
factorization with its remainder, for the translational state under interval
disturbances, verified against sampled multi-pass trajectories.
\end{openitem}

The second concerns what does not concentrate. The attitude and the elastic
motions are not events. They are oscillations, at the short-period attitude
and first structural periods $T_{\mathrm{att}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{ela}}$,
measured against the slow time $T_{\mathrm{slow}} = \sqrt{R_e/g}$ by
$\eps_{\mathrm{att}} = T_{\mathrm{att}}/T_{\mathrm{slow}}$ and
$\eps_{\mathrm{ela}} = T_{\mathrm{ela}}/T_{\mathrm{slow}}$, and they persist
for as long as there is air. The same holds for the oscillation about the
glide whenever the loads are bounded. In a profile decomposition, motions of
this kind are not profiles. They remain in the remainder, which the
wave-equation theory is careful to say is not small in energy, since it
contains every wave that concentrating profiles cannot
represent~\cite[p.~136]{bahouri1999high}. The tool for them is
averaging~\cite{khalil2002nonlinear,sanders2007averaging}: each oscillation
is carried by a slowly varying amplitude, and the slower dynamics see it
through its mean.

Two difficulties stand between this tool and a bound. First, an averaging
theorem gives the error to an order, and an order is not a bound, as the
standard text says of its own
estimates~\cite[Sec.~10.1]{khalil2002nonlinear}. Second, with several
oscillations the theorem holds only away from resonance, and the frequencies
here are not constants. The attitude frequency grows with the square root of
the dynamic pressure, so it rises from zero at the start of every pass, and
all the frequencies are known only to within the aerodynamic intervals.
Where two frequencies cross, averaging over both phases fails in a zone
about the resonance. The treatment there is itself an inner expansion
matched to an outer one, and a complete theory is not available even for a
single smooth field~\cite[Chs.~7--8]{sanders2007averaging}. The results that
pass through a resonance without resolving it hold, where capture is
possible, outside a set of initial conditions of small measure, and an outer
bound can discard nothing. This is the one genuinely new obligation for
which no theory is at hand, and it sits on the critical path.

\begin{openitem}{Separation of scales under interval disturbances}
\label{obj:averaging}
The framework separates three scales: atmospheric, attitude and elastic. The
oscillations that do not concentrate, namely the oscillation about the
glide, that of the attitude and that of the structure, must be averaged with
an error that is explicit, uniform over the selections of the interval
field, and valid for every trajectory, including those that pass through a
resonance. We must prove an averaging theorem with constants. The amplitude
of each oscillation is carried as a slow coordinate of the limit system of
\cref{sec:bound} or enclosed in the interval field, and the theorem bounds
the distance between the slow coordinates of the full system and those of
the averaged inclusion in closed form. The interval widths enter twice: as a
disturbance of each oscillation, and through the frequencies, which depend
on the uncertain coefficients, so that the ordering of the frequency bands
is itself a hypothesis to be verified over the corridor. Where two bands
cross, the statement is for the coupled pair, carried jointly through the
crossing, with the width of the resonance zone and the change of amplitude
across it enclosed. The objective is met by the theorem for the oscillation
about the glide and for the attitude, with the remainder in closed form, and
with the elastic modes either included or enclosed as a widened interval, as
in the fallback of \cref{sec:plan}.
\end{openitem}

\subsection{The certified bound}\label{sec:bound}
The skeleton and the bubbles together are not a surface with corrections but
a system of its own, with flows and jumps, and we state the target against
it. In the slow coordinates, the \emph{limit system} $\mathcal{H}_\delta$
has two flows and one jump. On an arc the state moves under gravity alone.
On the glide it moves along the balance $\Lambda = 1$, under the admissible
bank histories and the selections of the interval field, and it carries one
additional coordinate, the amplitude of the oscillation about the balance. A
pass that dissipates more than the threshold~$\delta$ of \cref{eq:kmax} is a
jump: the state that enters it is sent to the set $J(x)$ of states that can
leave it, onto an arc or onto the glide, with the time and the range
consumed by the pass included, and a trajectory makes at most $K_\delta$
jumps. The target statement is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target}
  \calR_\eps(T)
  \;\subseteq\;
  \underbrace{\Pi\bigl(\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}(T)\bigr)}_{\text{skeleton and bubbles}}
  \;\oplus\;
  \underbrace{\B\bigl(\varrho(\eps,\delta)\bigr)}_{\text{residual}},
\end{equation}
uniformly over the admissible family generated by the uncertainty set, not
along a nominal trajectory. Here $\calR_\eps(T)$ is the set of slow states
reachable at time~$T$ from the initial set, under every admissible control
and every selection of the interval field, and
$\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}(T)$ is the corresponding set for the limit
system. The map $\Pi$ reads a state of the limit system back in the
coordinates of the full one: it is the identity on an arc, and on the glide
it returns the altitudes and flight-path angles that an oscillation of the
carried amplitude visits. The symbol $\oplus$ denotes the Minkowski sum, and
$\B(\varrho)$ is the ball of radius~$\varrho$ in the corridor metric, a
weighted supremum norm in which altitude is measured in scale heights and
each remaining slow coordinate in units of its corridor width. Passes
compose in sequence, as jumps do, and their effects are not added, since an
arc that meets the atmosphere has no continuation without the pass.
\Cref{fig:target} illustrates the statement.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/reachable-set.pdf}
  \caption{Illustration of the target statement~\cref{eq:target}, drawn on
    the ground. The trajectory is the example of \cref{fig:skip}, with
    altitude drawn twelve times too large and a post marking each minute.
    The three sets are an illustration and are not computed: the set that
    the vehicle can reach, the set that the limit system can reach, and the
    latter thickened by the residual, which is the bound. The first may
    leave the second, but by no more than the residual.}
  \label{fig:target}
\end{figure}

\Cref{eq:target} is a statement about sets. As a statement about measures,
it is the claim of \cref{obj:embedding}: the occupation measures converge to
measures that satisfy the Liouville equation of $\mathcal{H}_\delta$, with
the mass that crosses each pass carried by a measure on the graph of~$J$.
The dual pair $(\cert,\certT)$ of \cref{sec:embedding}, non-decreasing along
the two flows and across the jump, is the form in which an outer bound on
$\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}(T)$ is certified.

The glide must carry an amplitude because of the dynamics. Let $z$ denote
the altitude above the glide in scale heights, and let $G = g - V^2/r$ be
the acceleration that the lift has to balance. Lift falls off exponentially
with altitude and $G$ does not, so to leading order in $\eps$, at frozen
speed and with the damping neglected,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscillator-body}
  \ddot z \;=\; \frac{G}{H_s}\,\bigl(e^{-z} - 1\bigr) .
\end{equation}
The glide is the equilibrium of this equation. The phugoid, the oscillation
of altitude against flight-path angle about the glide, is its small
oscillation, with frequency $\sqrt{G/H_s}$, which grows like
$\eps^{-1/2}$. A skip is its large oscillation: a rebound from the
exponential wall below and a ballistic arc under $-G$ above, which is how
the classical theory describes skip paths, as oscillatory paths of large
amplitude~\cite{shi1971skip}. An oscillation that crosses the glide altitude
at a flight-path angle $\gamma_0$ has an amplitude fixed by
$\gamma_0^2/\eps$, the parameter of the scaling in \cref{sec:loss}. The two
limits distinguished there are thus the two ends of one family, and the
threshold~$\delta$ is a cut in the amplitude: an oscillation above it is
resolved pass by pass, and one below it is averaged.

The terms neglected in \cref{eq:oscillator-body} decide the analysis. The
damping rate of the phugoid is $g/[V(L/D)\cos\sigma]$. It does not depend on
$\eps$, while the frequency grows like $\eps^{-1/2}$. In the limit, the
motion normal to the glide is therefore an undamped oscillation on its own
time scale. The glide balance is not a normally hyperbolic slow manifold in
the sense required by the geometric theory~\cite{fenichel1979geometric}, and
no boundary-layer estimate of Tikhonov type brings a trajectory onto it.
Moreover, the amplitude is not small. A change of bank moves the glide
altitude by a fixed fraction of a scale height, however thin the layer, and
a bank history in step with the oscillation pumps it, just as one opposed to
it damps it. Guidance laws use the latter~\cite{lu2014entry}, and a
reachable set must allow for the former. The amplitude is therefore a
coordinate on which the control acts, and its range belongs to
$\calR_{\mathcal{H}_\delta}$, not to the residual.

The residual $\varrho(\eps,\delta)$ collects what the limit system does not
carry. It has three parts: the lag of a trajectory behind the descending
glide, the matching error at each of at most $K_\delta$ passes, and the
averaging error of the oscillations. The residual and the count $K_\delta$
are to be derived as explicit, closed-form expressions in the corridor
bounds and the aerodynamic interval widths, independent of any numerical
optimization. One piece is immediate: the count is given by
\cref{eq:kmax}. The rest must be derived. For the lag, we expect a bound of
order $\sqrt{\eps}$ in scale heights, the order of the damping ratio of the
phugoid, with a constant depending on the speed, the lift-to-drag ratio and
the bank angle that degenerates as $V$ approaches circular speed, where the
glide gives way to arcs and passes. Such a bound describes the ringing
amplitude of a trajectory started level on the glide and nothing more. It
says nothing about a trajectory that leaves a pass, which rings with
whatever amplitude the pass left it, for a time comparable to the entry.
That amplitude is what the limit system carries.

In the spherical-LVLH coordinates the altitude and speed bounds of the
corridor are box constraints on $(r,V)$. The heating, dynamic-pressure and
load limits are not: each is a curve $h \ge h_{\min}(V)$. Each is, however,
monotone in altitude and in speed at fixed attitude, so contracting a box
against it returns the exact interval hull of the feasible part, and the
interval widths that enter $\varrho$ and $K_\delta$ inherit that tightness.
Requiring the constants in closed form is not what makes the bound a proof,
since a verified solver output is one too (\cref{sec:provenance}). It is
what makes the dependence on the corridor and on the interval widths
explicit, so that the bound is evaluated again, and not solved for again,
when either changes.

\begin{openitem}{Analytical derivation of the corridor constants}
\label{obj:constants}
The guarantee rests on the count $K_\delta$ and the residual
$\varrho(\eps,\delta)$. We must derive both as explicit functions of the
corridor bounds, the initial set and the aerodynamic interval widths,
without relying on any numerical solver. The count follows from the energy
budget, with the threshold $\delta$ chosen against the residual that it
leaves behind: lowering it resolves more passes one by one and pays a
matching error for each, while raising it leaves larger oscillations to be
averaged. The residual has three parts: the lag behind the glide, the
matching error of \cref{obj:composition}, and the averaging error of
\cref{obj:averaging}. Each constant must hold for every selection of the
interval field, and its range of validity, bounded away from circular speed,
is part of the statement. The objective is met by formulas whose evaluation
over the corridor in validated interval arithmetic bounds the distance from
sampled trajectories of the full model to the reachable set of the limit
system, with no fitted number.
\end{openitem}

\section{The application: hypersonic entry}\label{sec:application}
%==========================================================================
Entry is not an arbitrary illustration of the machinery above. It supplies
the singular perturbation, the limit system and the concentrating events in
one system, and, for the reasons that follow, the existing methods stop
short of it. The vehicle and its flight corridor enter the framework only as
data, so the bound is sought for hypersonic entry vehicles in general and
not for a particular one.

\subsection{Why existing methods do not reach it}
The direct method solves a Hamilton--Jacobi--Isaacs equation on a grid,
which is exponential in the state dimension and capped in practice near four
or five states~\parencite{bansal2017hamilton,mitchell2005time}. A rigid-body
entry vehicle carries thirteen states,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:state-rigid}
  x = (r,\lambda,\phi,\, V,\gamma,\psi,\,
       q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3,\, p,q_b,r_b)
  \;\in\;\R^{13},
\end{equation}
and more once the heat load, the mass, the thermal response and recession of
the heat shield and the aerothermoelastic modes are
coupled~\parencite{mcnamara2011aeroelastic}. The two counts use ``degree of
freedom'' in its two customary senses. The vehicle has six degrees of
freedom in the configuration sense (three translational, three rotational),
while \cref{eq:state-rigid} has thirteen coordinates spanning a
twelve-dimensional state manifold once the unit-quaternion constraint
$\norm{\vvec{q}} = 1$ is imposed. So the exact grid method quits before the
real problem starts.

Set-propagation methods go further. Taylor-model and zonotope flowpipes
return rigorous outer bounds at ten to twenty states, and they are the
baseline against which this program must be
measured~\cite{chen2013flowstar,althoff2021set,makino2003taylor}. What they
return is an enclosure of one instance: a numerical set for one vehicle, one
initial box and one horizon, whose tightness tends to degrade with the width
of the initial set and with the length of weakly damped motion that it has
to follow, and the phugoid is weakly damped for the whole of the glide. They
are the right tool inside a pass, where the horizon is short, and this is
how \cref{obj:passmaps} uses them. They do not produce a bound whose
dependence on the corridor and on the interval widths is explicit, which is
what a statement uniform over a family requires.

In practice the question is answered by sampling. Entry guidance has
converged on numerical predictor--corrector algorithms whose performance is
established by simulation over dispersed cases~\cite{lu2014entry}, which is
a sample of the family and not an enclosure of it.

What makes the measure-theoretic route viable is not that it evades the
dimension but that the bound it produces does not scale with it: the limit
system is low-dimensional by construction, each pass enters through one map,
and the residual is a single ball.

\subsection{The state space and the corridor}
We carry the translational state in geocentric spherical position
$(r,\lambda,\phi)$ and wind-frame velocity $(V,\gamma,\psi)$. In these
coordinates the altitude and speed bounds of the entry corridor are box
constraints on states, and the heating, dynamic-pressure and load limits are
monotone in the two coordinates that they bind, which is the natural setting
for interval contraction. Attitude is a unit quaternion, free of Euler-angle
singularities. The chart has a price. The longitude and heading rates are
singular at the geographic poles, so we take the corridor with
$\abs{\phi}\le\phi_{\max}<\pi/2$, and a trajectory class that overflies a
pole needs a second chart or a Cartesian formulation.

Because interval arithmetic evaluates transcendentals natively, the
algebraic adjunctions that recast the system in polynomial
form~\cite{savageau1987recasting} are not needed on the main line; they
survive only inside the moment-sum-of-squares cross-check. The state space
therefore carries only the physical dimensions (spherical position,
wind-frame velocity, attitude quaternion, body rates, thermal, ablation, and
modal states), with no artificial embeddings. In particular, the two
auxiliary states of the polynomial formulation, the density root
$y = \sqrt{\rho/\rho_0}$ and the wall-temperature surrogate, are eliminated.
This is a design decision rather than an open problem, and its trade-off is
the following: transcendental dynamics in coordinates native to the corridor
and the glide, against polynomial dynamics in which the corridor is a
nonlinear function of the state.

\begin{openitem}{Independent verification}
\label{obj:verification}
The certificate must be shown to be correct by evidence that does not share
its derivation. We must carry out three checks, in the order of
\cref{sec:provenance}: symbolic verification of every derivation behind the
constants; a moment-sum-of-squares outer approximation on a reduced
polynomial model, as a cross-check that shares neither the decomposition nor
the interval arithmetic; and a comparison on a common benchmark, a capsule
skip entry reconstructed from published data, against Taylor-model flowpipes
and against sampled trajectories of the full model, reported for each
coordinate group rather than as a single number. The objective is met by the
containment of every sample in the certified set, agreement with the
cross-check where both apply, and a stated tightness against the baseline,
coordinate by coordinate.
\end{openitem}

\section{Plan of work}\label{sec:plan}
%==========================================================================
Seven concrete obligations carry the program. They are stated where each
arises above, and we gather them here, in order, as the plan of work.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Liouville equation for the interval inclusion}: what the
    occupation measures of the interval inclusion converge to, and the
    Liouville constraint, with its jump term, that the limit satisfies.
  \item \emph{Validated evaluation of the certificate}: interval evaluation,
    contraction and integration that discharge the certificate with every
    rounding error accounted for, and that enclose the reachable set of the
    limit system.
  \item \emph{Wrapping-effect suppression in boundary-layer bubbles}: tight
    enclosures carried through a sequence of atmospheric passes without
    premature blow-up.
  \item \emph{Composition of passes}: the factorization of a trajectory
    through the pass maps and the flows between them, with an explicit
    matching remainder.
  \item \emph{Separation of scales under interval disturbances}: an
    averaging theorem with constants, uniform over the selections and valid
    where frequencies cross. This is \textbf{the critical path}, on which
    the reduction to a limit system and the closed-form bound rest.
  \item \emph{Analytical derivation of the corridor constants}: the count
    $K_\delta$ and the residual $\varrho(\eps,\delta)$ from the physical
    corridor and the interval widths, free of any numerical solver.
  \item \emph{Independent verification}: symbolic checks of the derivations,
    a moment-sum-of-squares cross-check, and a benchmark against
    set-propagation tools and sampled trajectories.
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{obj:embedding} states the limit;
\cref{obj:passmaps,obj:composition,obj:averaging} establish the
decomposition; \cref{obj:constants} reduces it to closed form; and
\cref{obj:pipeline,obj:verification} compute with it and check it.
\Cref{obj:averaging} gates the rest. The central theorem is open, and no
part of this program is claimed as done.

\subsection{Sequencing}
The dependency structure suggests three phases rather than seven parallel
tracks. The first phase establishes the embedding, that is,
\cref{obj:embedding} together with the certified envelope of the atmospheric
density that it needs in order to be stated on a concrete inclusion. The
second phase attacks the critical path, \cref{obj:averaging}. This is where
the program either works or does not, so it should be reached early enough
that a negative result is still recoverable. Its first case is small and
self-contained: the planar oscillator of \cref{eq:oscillator-body}, with its
damping, its drift, and a bank history acting on it.
\Cref{obj:passmaps,obj:composition} run alongside, since the matching
argument and the enclosure geometry constrain each other. The third phase
reduces the decomposition to closed form (\cref{obj:constants}), completes
the validated evaluation of \cref{obj:pipeline}, and carries out the
symbolic and moment-sum-of-squares checks of \cref{obj:verification}. The
validated evaluation is the one part of the program that a weaker theorem
does not shorten.

We identify two retreat positions now. If \cref{obj:averaging} resists a
proof where frequencies cross, the fallback is a corridor on which the
frequency bands are ordered (a floor on the dynamic pressure and a ceiling
on the speed below circular), with the crossings at the edges of each pass
left to the pass enclosures of \cref{obj:passmaps}, which integrate the
coupled field and need no separation, and with the elastic modes treated as
a widened interval. This weakens the residual without invalidating the
decomposition. If the amplitude of the oscillation about the glide cannot be
bounded over every bank history, we state the result for bank histories that
vary slowly compared with the oscillation, for which the amplitude decays.
This restricts the family and keeps the bound explicit.
